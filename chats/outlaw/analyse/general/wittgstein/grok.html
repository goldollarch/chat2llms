Language Games and the Limits of Judicial Meaning: A Wittgensteinian Critique of the Chen Jingyuan Case

Ludwig Wittgenstein’s philosophical oeuvre, spanning his early Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) and later Philosophical Investigations (1953), revolves around the nature of language as the scaffold of our world. In the Tractatus, Wittgenstein posits a picture theory of language: propositions mirror atomic facts in a logical structure, delineating what can be said clearly from the mystical realm of the unsayable—“Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.” This early atomism gives way in his later work to a pluralistic view of “language games,” where meaning emerges not from fixed essences but from use within social practices, governed by rules that are family resemblances rather than rigid definitions. Concepts like “truth,” “falsehood,” or “disorder” have no universal core but overlap in overlapping similarities, shaped by context and community. Wittgenstein’s private language argument further underscores that meaning requires public criteria; isolated “private” senses dissolve into incoherence. Applied to Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪)—a vague “pocket crime” for forwarding low-engagement Twitter posts—the case reveals a profound misuse of language: the judiciary’s imposition of a rigid, state-centric “language game” on Chen’s scholarly practice, silencing the multiplicity of meanings and enforcing a mystical “order” through inarticulate power.

Chen, an independent physicist specializing in complex systems, was arrested in Kunming in September 2022 for sharing posts on his account (@_cenjoy), which boasted zero followers and fewer than 100 reposts total. These included artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing protest resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical discussions (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical snippets (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). The court, under Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun, “sorted” these as “false information” that “insulted national leaders and the political system,” causing “serious public disorder.” No evidence of impact was presented—no quantifiable disruption, no societal ripple—yet the presumption hinged on Chen’s “high education” implying he “knew” the posts were subversive. Wittgenstein would diagnose this as a failure of linguistic clarity: the Tractatus’s admonition against nonsense propositions is violated when “disorder” is strung together without picturing any atomic fact. The charges form a pseudo-proposition, a mystical incantation (“Whereof one cannot speak”—of actual harm—“thereof one must be silent”), masking the judiciary’s inability to articulate a coherent picture of crime.

In Wittgenstein’s later turn to language games, the case unfolds as a clash of incompatible practices. Chen’s forwards were moves in an academic “game”: posts as tools for inquiry, their “meaning” derived from use—collecting fragments for complex systems analysis, evoking family resemblances among art (symbolic play), emotion (expressive release), theory (debative exploration), and history (factual assembly). Wittgenstein’s notion of rule-following as communal practice fits: Chen, as a solitary scholar, participated in the global “game” of intellectual exchange, where “truth” or “insult” emerges from contextual overlaps, not essence. A cartoon’s “umbrella girl” resembles protest symbols in one game (artistic commentary) but not in another’s (state security lexicon). The judiciary, however, enforces a different game: the “official” language of “national harmony,” where posts are not tools but threats, their use predetermined as subversive. This is not misunderstanding but power: the court’s “sorting” (梳理) imposes a rulebook where Chen’s private, low-stakes practice is retrofitted into a public spectacle of disorder. Wittgenstein warns against such “bewitchment” by language—here, the bewitchment of “rumor” (谣言) as a family resemblance stretched to encompass everything non-conforming, dissolving criteria into arbitrary fiat.

The private language argument sharpens this critique. Chen’s defense invokes subjective limits—Gödel’s incompleteness theorem and Humean skepticism—to argue he could not “know” posts as false in an uncertain world. Wittgenstein would concur: “knowledge” of falsity requires public benchmarks, not private intuition; isolated “mingzhi” (明知, knowing awareness) is incoherent without shared games. The court’s presumption—“high education = malice”—treats Chen’s mind as a private ostensive definition, unverifiable and thus meaningless. Worse, procedural opacity (non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison letter) enforces silence on the very language needed for rebuttal, turning justice into an unsayable mysticism. Selective enforcement—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—exposes the game’s rigged rules: the “they” (das Man) of official discourse plays by one set, while dissidents play another, forever mismatched.

Wittgenstein’s therapeutic philosophy—dissolving philosophical problems by clarifying language—offers no resolution but illumination. Chen’s Prison Blood Letter, vowing “life without end, struggle without cease” and lifelong accountability, enacts this: not a grand theory but a patient unraveling of judicial nonsense, exposing “disorder” as a misfired proposition. In a world of fragmented games, his case warns of the dangers when one practice (state security) colonizes others (scholarly inquiry), breeding the very confusions it claims to cure. Wittgenstein ends the Investigations with a plea for peace in diversity: “My propositions serve as elucidations… He who understands me finally recognizes them as nonsensical.” Chen’s ordeal, and his defiant writings, elucidate the absurdity of imposed meanings, inviting us to play our games with care—lest silence befall the sayable.
