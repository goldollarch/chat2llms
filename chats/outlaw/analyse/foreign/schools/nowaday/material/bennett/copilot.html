From Jane Bennett’s New Materialist perspective, the case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan reveals how political resistance is not solely a matter of human will, but a distributed, affective, and material phenomenon. His silence is not inert—it is vibrant, charged with ethical force, and entangled with the materialities of law, state power, and public memory.

1. Vibrant Matter: Silence as a Political Force

Bennett’s central idea in Vibrant Matter is that matter is not passive or inert, but alive with affective and agential capacities. Dr. Chen’s silence—his refusal to confess or participate in the state’s narrative—is not merely symbolic. It is a form of vibrant matter, a material act that resists capture and disrupts the flow of institutional power.

His silence reverberates through prison walls, legal documents, and digital networks. It becomes a force-field, unsettling the apparatus that seeks to render him legible and compliant.

2. Assemblages of Resistance

Bennett emphasizes that agency is not located in discrete individuals but emerges from assemblages—dynamic constellations of human and non-human actors. Dr. Chen’s resistance is not his alone. It is co-produced by the courtroom, the prison cell, the legal code, the pen he refused to use, the paper he refused to sign, and the public who bears witness.

This assemblage of resistance is not linear or hierarchical—it is affective, material, and contingent. It shows how even a single act of refusal can ripple through a system, not because of its volume, but because of its vibrancy.

3. Affect and the Ethics of Attention

Bennett’s work calls for an “ecological” ethics—one that attends to the affective and material forces that shape political life. Dr. Chen’s case is not just a legal matter; it is an affective event. His silence generates unease, sorrow, admiration, and solidarity. These affects are not secondary—they are political energies that animate resistance and memory.

To attend to his silence is to practice an ethics of attention—to feel the weight of what is not said, and to recognize the moral force of stillness.

4. Disrupting the Machinery of Governance

In Bennett’s view, power is not only repressive but also infrastructural—it operates through mundane materials, routines, and systems. Dr. Chen’s refusal to participate in the legal performance disrupts this machinery. His silence is a jam in the gears, a refusal to lubricate the smooth functioning of coercion.

By not speaking, he forces the system to speak louder, to reveal its own violence, and in doing so, he amplifies the ethical stakes of the moment.

5. Conclusion: Silence as Vibrant Ethics

From Jane Bennett’s New Materialist perspective, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s silence is not absence—it is vibrant presence. It is a material, affective, and ethical act that resists reduction, resists erasure, and resists the flattening of political life into obedience.

“There is a kind of vitality intrinsic to materiality.” — Jane Bennett, Vibrant Matter

Dr. Chen’s silence is such vitality. It pulses with resistance, not as a cry for recognition, but as a refusal to be assimilated into the machinery of untruth.
