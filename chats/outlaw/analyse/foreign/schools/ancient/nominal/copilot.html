From the standpoint of Nominalism, the case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan reveals the dangers of treating abstract legal categories as if they possess intrinsic reality. His silence resists being subsumed under generalized labels like “criminal” or “troublemaker,” asserting instead the irreducible particularity of his situation. Nominalism reminds us that universals do not exist independently of the individuals they claim to describe—and that justice must begin with the singular.

1. Universals as Fictions: The Violence of Legal Abstraction

Nominalism holds that only particular things exist; universals are mere names, not entities. In Dr. Chen’s case, the charge of “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” functions as a universal abstraction—a vague category applied to diverse individuals to suppress dissent. The law treats this label as if it were a real essence, ignoring the specificity of Dr. Chen’s actions, motives, and ethical stance.

His silence is a rejection of this abstraction. It insists: “I am not a type—I am a singular being.”

2. Justice Begins with the Particular

Nominalism demands that we attend to individuals, not categories. Dr. Chen’s case cannot be understood through generalizations about “social stability” or “public order.” It must be approached through the concrete facts of his speech, his silence, his legal reasoning, and his ethical refusal.

To do otherwise is to commit the nominalist critique of injustice: treating names as things, and thereby erasing the human.

3. Naming as Power: The Politics of Classification

In a nominalist frame, naming is not neutral—it is a political act. The state’s power lies in its ability to name: to classify dissent as disorder, critique as provocation, silence as guilt. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess is a refusal to be named into submission.

His silence is a nominalist resistance—a refusal to accept the reality of imposed universals. He asserts that truth cannot be captured by labels, and that justice must be grounded in the particular.

4. Conclusion: Singular Resistance Against Universal Fiction

From a nominalist perspective, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s case is a confrontation between singularity and abstraction, between the lived reality of a dissenter and the fictive universals of authoritarian legality. His silence is not emptiness—it is a philosophical protest against the reduction of life to names.

“There are no crimes in general, only acts in particular.” — Nominalist ethos

Dr. Chen’s silence affirms this truth. It is not a refusal to speak—it is a refusal to be misnamed.
