The Chen Jingyuan Case: A Razian Critique of Judicial Authority and Autonomy

Joseph Raz’s legal philosophy, particularly as developed in The Authority of Law (1979) and The Morality of Freedom (1986), offers a compelling framework for evaluating the legitimacy of legal systems through the lens of authority and personal autonomy. For Raz, law’s claim to authority is not absolute but conditional: it derives from its capacity to provide “exclusionary reasons” for action—directives that not only guide behavior but also preempt conflicting considerations from other sources, thereby enhancing individual autonomy by simplifying decision-making in a complex world. Legitimate law must be grounded in moral principles, serving the well-being of its subjects without arbitrary interference. Raz emphasizes that authority is justified only if it is likely to better track reasons for action than individual deliberation alone, and it must respect autonomy as the capacity for meaningful choice. Excessive or irrational coercion undermines this, rendering law a tool of domination rather than guidance. The 2023 conviction of Dr. Chen Jingyuan for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) in Kunming, China, starkly illustrates the perversion of legal authority: a scholar’s innocuous online activity is criminalized through opaque procedures, eroding autonomy and exposing the fragility of exclusionary claims in an overreaching judiciary.

At its core, Chen’s case involves forwarding a handful of Twitter posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy)—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical discussions (e.g., political spectrum analyses, evaluations of Xi Jinping, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, retired cadre pay slips, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). With zero followers and fewer than 100 reposts, these acts had negligible impact, yet Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun “sorted” them as “false information” insulting national leaders and causing “serious public disorder.” Raz would view this as a profound failure of legal authority: the law’s exclusionary force—demanding obedience without robust justification—does not enhance autonomy but annihilates it. In Raz’s terms, for a directive to claim authority, it must be more likely than not to advance the subject’s reasons for action (e.g., truth-seeking or cultural engagement). Here, the vague “pocket crime” lacks such grounding: no evidence of falsity (no appraisals), intent (no proof of “knowledge” beyond presuming “high education” as malice), or harm (zero societal ripple), rendering the conviction an arbitrary imposition that preempts Chen’s deliberative freedom rather than aiding it.

Raz’s emphasis on autonomy as the moral foundation of law further indicts the procedural injustices. Autonomy, for Raz, is not mere liberty but the effective capacity to make choices that reflect one’s values—enhanced by law’s guidance but undermined by coercion without rationale. Chen, an independent scholar of complex systems, exercised autonomy through private inquiry: forwarding posts as a means to preserve and critique diverse ideas, aligning with his essence as a truth-seeker. The non-public trial, denial of full defense, suppression of his prison complaint letter, and selective enforcement (state media republished similar content unscathed) exemplify Raz’s critique of “naked power”: law as domination, not service. By presuming guilt from education (“high knowledge implies malice”), the judiciary inverts autonomy’s purpose—turning intellectual capacity, a Razian good, into a vice. This echoes Raz’s warning in The Morality of Freedom that authority loses legitimacy when it systematically frustrates well-being; here, it fosters alienation, reducing Chen to a passive object of state will.

Moreover, the case challenges Raz’s “service conception” of authority: law must serve subjects’ interests, not merely the ruler’s. The “serious disorder” claim—fabricated from whispers in the digital void—fails Raz’s test of efficacy: it neither resolves conflicts nor promotes collective autonomy but enforces conformity, preempting the very deliberation (e.g., on global ideas) that enriches society. Raz argues that legitimate exclusionary reasons must be “protected” by moral constraints; Kunming’s opacity and presumption violate this, transforming law into a “peremptory” barrier against reason.

In conclusion, Razian philosophy exposes Chen’s case as a betrayal of legal authority’s promise: instead of empowering autonomy through justified guidance, it wields exclusionary force as arbitrary coercion. This not only wrongs an individual—imprisoning a scholar for thought’s faint echoes—but erodes systemic legitimacy, as citizens withdraw from the deliberative public sphere. Reforming such practices—through transparent evidence, respect for inquiry, and autonomy-enhancing rules—would realign law with Raz’s vision: a servant of human flourishing, not its jailer. Chen’s resilient defense, demanding accountability, embodies the moral autonomy Raz champions, a quiet rebuke to the overreaching “they.”
