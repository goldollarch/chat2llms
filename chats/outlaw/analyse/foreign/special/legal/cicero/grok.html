Cicero’s Eternal Justice and the Chen Jingyuan Affair: A Roman Lens on Modern Injustice

In the grand theater of human affairs, where the scales of justice must ever balance the weight of law against the frailty of power, Marcus Tullius Cicero stands as an eternal sentinel. The great Roman orator and philosopher, in his De Legibus (On the Laws), proclaimed that “true law is right reason in agreement with nature… universal, unchanging, everlasting… it is not one thing at Rome and another at Athens.” For Cicero, law is not the arbitrary decree of tyrants but the art of the good and the just (ars boni et aequi), rooted in natural equity, divine order, and the rational soul’s pursuit of virtue. It demands an independent judiciary, impartial trials, and the sacred rights of citizens—freedom of speech, presumption of innocence, and protection from caprice. These principles, forged in the crucible of Rome’s republican struggles, illuminate the shadows of Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s case: a scholar ensnared by a judicial farce in 2023, convicted of “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) for forwarding innocuous Twitter posts. Through Cicero’s eyes, this affair is not mere error but a betrayal of natural law, a descent from republic to despotism, where the voice of reason is silenced by the clamor of authority.

Consider the facts, as Cicero would urge in his forensic eloquence: Dr. Chen, a humble physicist from rural Yunnan, devoted his life to the mysteries of complex systems—astronomy, neuroscience, artificial intelligence, and social dynamics. In 2019, he returned home to tend his aging parents, living as an independent scholar, his Twitter account (@_cenjoy) a quiet repository of inquiry. With zero followers and fewer than 100 reposts over years, he shared fragments of human discourse: artistic cartoons like the “umbrella girl,” symbolizing resilience amid protest; emotional memorials such as June 4th candlelight images, voicing subjective grief; theoretical debates on political spectra, Trump’s critique of communism, or Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks, fostering intellectual exchange; and historical records like edits to Mao’s Selected Works, retired cadre pay slips, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, or Sino-Ukrainian military ties—verifiable facts, not fabrications. No ripple of disorder followed; no public frenzy ensued. Yet, in September 2022, Kunming police stormed his lodging, charging him with disseminating “false information” that “insulted national leaders and the political system,” allegedly causing “serious public disorder.”

Cicero, defender of the Roman res publica, would decry this as the perversion of ius naturale—the eternal law binding all peoples. In De Re Publica, he warns that true justice flows from reason and equity, not the whims of the powerful: “The law is the bond of civil society, and the authority to make it belongs only to the wise.” Judge Pu Huijun’s presumption that Chen’s “high education” implies he “should discern” falsehoods, leading to a one-year-eight-month sentence without public trial or full defense, mocks this bond. Where is the equity? The posts—art’s metaphor, emotion’s cry, theory’s dialectic, history’s record—are not verifiable “rumors” but expressions of the human spirit, akin to Cicero’s own orations against Catiline: bold, contestatory, yet vital to the commonwealth. To brand them “disruptive” without evidence—no appraisals, no data on impact, no causation proven—is to wield law as a tyrant’s rod, not a guardian’s shield. Cicero thundered in his Pro Milone that justice requires not vengeance but deliberation: “Let us not confound right with might.” Kunming’s selective enforcement—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—exposes this confusion: equity yields to favoritism, natural law to arbitrary decree.

Worse still, the procedural shadows cast doubt on the republic’s soul. Cicero, architect of Roman jurisprudence, insisted in De Legibus on open courts and the presumption of innocence: “The safety of the people shall be the highest law” (salus populi suprema lex esto). Chen’s non-public hearing, denied self-defense, and suppressed prison complaint violate this supreme law. Prosecutor Ge Bin’s indictment and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun’s dismissal without rebuttal echo the Catilinarian conspiracies Cicero exposed: whispers of subversion amplified into treason by unchecked power. The “pocket crime” vagueness— a catch-all for dissent—betrays Cicero’s ideal of precise, rational statutes, devolving into the very “despotic license” he abhorred. In forwarding these posts, Chen exercised the citizen’s natural right to discourse, much as Cicero invoked free speech in the Forum to safeguard the res publica. To punish inquiry as “quarreling” is to chain the mind, rendering the law not a bond of society but a fetter on virtue.

Cicero’s vision of the just republic—where natural law tempers positive law, and magistrates serve equity over expediency—condemns this affair as a harbinger of decay. As he lamented in De Officiis, “The foundation of justice is good faith,” a virtue absent in Kunming’s “sorting” of evidence without transparency. Chen’s release in 2024, though shadowed by restrictions, underscores the case’s fragility: a scholar’s whispers posed no threat, yet elicited iron bars. This is not justice but farce, eroding the very republic it claims to protect. Cicero would call for reform: restore open trials, enforce evidentiary rigor, and enshrine speech as the lifeblood of citizenship. Only then can China’s “rule of law” echo Rome’s: not a tool of the mighty, but the eternal voice of reason.

In the end, Chen Jingyuan stands as Cicero’s modern echo—a defender of equity amid authoritarian drift. His Prison Blood Letter, vowing lifelong accountability for his accusers, embodies the orator’s resolve: “While there’s breath in my body, I shall speak for justice.” Let Kunming heed this call, lest the scales tip from res publica to res privata—the people’s republic to the private fiefdom of the few.
