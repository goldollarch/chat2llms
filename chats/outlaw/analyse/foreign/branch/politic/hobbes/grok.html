A Hobbesian Commentary on the Case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan: The Sovereign’s Sword and the Fragility of the Social Contract

Thomas Hobbes’s political philosophy, as masterfully expounded in Leviathan (1651), posits a stark vision of human nature and the necessities of order. In the state of nature—bereft of common power—life is “solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short,” a war of all against all (bellum omnium contra ommnes), driven by fear, competition, and glory. To escape this anarchy, individuals enter a social contract, surrendering natural rights to an absolute sovereign who wields undivided authority to enforce peace, interpret laws, and suppress threats. The sovereign is the “mortal god,” an artificial person whose will constitutes the law, ensuring security above all else. Liberty, for Hobbes, is not unfettered license but the freedom to act within the bounds of law—“the absence of external impediments to motion.” Dissent or disruption risks unraveling the contract, justifying swift, unyielding punishment. Yet, Hobbes warns that the sovereign’s power must inspire trust; if perceived as arbitrary, it breeds fear rather than allegiance, potentially fracturing the Leviathan itself. Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪)—stemming from forwarding innocuous Twitter posts—invokes this Hobbesian tension: a sovereign’s vigilant sword against perceived chaos, but one that risks overreach, eroding the very security it seeks to preserve.

Chen’s case unfolds in the shadow of this contractual order. An independent scholar with a Ph.D. in physics, specializing in complex systems, Chen forwarded posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy)—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). With near-zero followers and under 100 reposts, these acts generated no measurable disruption—zero engagement, no societal ripple. From a Hobbesian vantage, the sovereign’s response—prosecution by Ge Bin, conviction by Judge Pu Huijun, and upheld by appellate Judge Li Xiangyun—appears defensible: in a fragile peace, even whispers of “false information” or “insults to national leaders” could ignite the passions of the multitude, reverting society to natural war. The vague “pocket crime” statute serves as the sovereign’s preemptive blade, nipping potential sedition in the bud, much like Hobbes’s advocacy for censorship to curb “seditious doctrines” that might dissolve the commonwealth. Chen’s “high education” presumption as malice aligns with Hobbes’s realism: the learned, like ancient sophists, might wield words as weapons, justifying scrutiny to safeguard the contract.

Yet, Hobbes’s Leviathan is no unchecked tyranny; its legitimacy rests on delivering security, not instilling terror. Here, the case strains this foundation. The judiciary’s “sorting” of posts as “rumors” lacks empirical mooring—no appraisals, no data on impact, no causal links—fabricating “serious public disorder” from ether. Procedural shadows—a non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison letters—evoke not protective order but arbitrary caprice, breeding the very fear Hobbes abhors. Selective enforcement (state media republishing similar content unscathed) undermines impartiality, fracturing the contract’s mutuality: subjects surrender liberty for equal protection, yet Chen, a humble scholar, bears disproportionate burden while the powerful evade. Hobbes cautions that a sovereign perceived as partial invites rebellion; if the law becomes a tool of ressentiment rather than reason, it dissolves the artificial bonds holding the commonwealth. Chen’s forwards—art’s symbolism, emotion’s authenticity, theory’s contestation, history’s lessons—were not warlike but contemplative, echoing Hobbes’s own praise for philosophy as a private pursuit that, when harmless, enriches the state. Punishing them risks the absurd: a sovereign so vigilant against phantoms that it conjures the monsters it fears.

In Hobbesian terms, the case tests the social contract’s tensile strength. The sovereign’s absolute power is a necessary evil, but its exercise must foster trust, not alienation. Chen’s Prison Blood Letter—a vow of “lifelong struggle” and accountability for his accusers—embodies the subject’s residual liberty: even in chains, one affirms one’s covenantal role by demanding reciprocity. If the judiciary revalues such cases toward evidence and equity—aligning with Hobbes’s pragmatic end of peace—it fortifies the Leviathan. Otherwise, it courts the natural state’s return: not through Chen’s whispers, but through the erosive doubt of the governed. Hobbes reminds us: the sword protects the contract, but a blunted blade invites its rupture. In Chen’s quiet defiance, we glimpse the sovereign’s peril—and the subject’s enduring stake in order’s fragile dance.
