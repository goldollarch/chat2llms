Republicanism and the Chen Jingyuan Case: Pettit’s Non-Domination and the Erosion of Civic Liberty

Philip Pettit’s republicanism, as articulated in Republicanism: A Theory of Freedom and Government (1997), reimagines political freedom not as mere non-interference—the liberal ideal of being left alone—but as non-domination: the absence of arbitrary power over one’s choices. For Pettit, true liberty requires institutional safeguards that prevent any actor, public or private, from wielding unchecked sway, ensuring that citizens can contest decisions and live without the constant threat of capricious control. This demands a robust civic ethos, contestable institutions, and a balance of powers, where law serves as a vigilant sentinel against domination rather than a tool of it. In the 2023 case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan, convicted of “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) for forwarding innocuous Twitter posts, Pettit’s framework illuminates a profound republican failure: a judicial apparatus that, far from safeguarding non-domination, becomes its very embodiment, subjecting a scholar to arbitrary state power and undermining the contestability essential to republican citizenship.

Chen’s offense—sharing posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy) about art (e.g., the “umbrella girl” cartoon symbolizing resilience), emotion (e.g., June 4th candlelight memorials), theory (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and history (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement)—was neither disruptive nor influential. With near-zero followers and fewer than 100 reposts, these acts posed no tangible threat, evoking no societal ripple. Yet Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun “sorted” them as “false information” insulting leaders and causing “serious public disorder,” imposing an 20-month sentence without public trial, full defense rights, or evidence of impact. This is domination in Pettit’s starkest sense: arbitrary power unchecked by contestability. The vague “pocket crime” statute allows officials to impose will without justification, reducing Chen to an object of state caprice—his scholarly inquiry, a private exercise of judgment, reclassified as subversion. Pettit warns that such “uncontestable domination” breeds servility, eroding the civic virtue needed for republican self-government. Here, the judiciary’s opacity—suppressed prison letter, selective enforcement (state media republished similar content unscathed)—exemplifies this: citizens cannot deliberate or appeal, fostering a culture of fear rather than robust participation.

Pettit’s institutional republicanism prescribes remedies: laws must be contestable, with mechanisms like independent oversight and transparent procedures to prevent arbitrary sway. In Chen’s case, the non-public hearing and unproven “disorder” (zero quantifiable harm) violate this, turning law into a dominator rather than liberator. Pettit, drawing from Roman republicanism, advocates “empire of law” over “empire of men”—yet Kunming’s process inverts this, prioritizing officials’ “sorting” over evidence. Chen’s Prison Blood Letter, vowing lifelong accountability for his accusers, embodies Pettit’s ideal of civic contestation: a resolute challenge to domination, affirming non-subservient agency. His Gödel-inspired admission of knowledge limits further aligns with Pettit’s emphasis on fallible human judgment, underscoring the need for deliberative humility in institutions.

Ultimately, Chen’s ordeal critiques a system where republican ideals falter: non-domination requires not just formal rights but their lived contestability. By subjecting intellectual freedom to arbitrary fiat, the judiciary risks republican decay—citizens, fearing the gaze of power, withdraw from public life. Pettit’s vision demands reform: embed contestability in law (e.g., mandatory public scrutiny for vague offenses), foster civic virtue through education, and institutionalize checks against judicial overreach. Chen’s case, a microcosm of this failure, calls for a republican renewal: only by curbing domination can China realize Pettit’s free republic, where scholars like Chen debate without dread. In this light, Chen’s unyielding spirit is not defiance but the essence of republican liberty—contesting power to reclaim it for all.
