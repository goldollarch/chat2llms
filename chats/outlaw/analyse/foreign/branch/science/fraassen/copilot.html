Evaluating the case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan through the lens of Bas van Fraassen’s Constructive Empiricism reveals a critical tension between observable phenomena and the state’s demand for metaphysical conformity. Van Fraassen’s philosophy, articulated in The Scientific Image, insists that science need only aim for empirical adequacy, not metaphysical truth. Applying this to legal and political contexts, we find that Dr. Chen’s resistance—his refusal to confess, his insistence on reasoned critique—embodies a kind of epistemic modesty that stands in stark contrast to the state’s metaphysical overreach.

1. Empirical Adequacy vs. Ideological Totality

Van Fraassen argues that a scientific theory is acceptable if it adequately accounts for observable phenomena. It need not explain everything; it must only fit the data. In Dr. Chen’s case, his speech and silence are observable acts. The state’s interpretation—that these acts constitute “provocation” or “disorder”—is not empirically adequate:

  • There is no observable disruption caused by his speech.

  • The causal link between his behavior and alleged harm is speculative, not empirically grounded.

  • The legal theory used against him demands belief in unobservable political metaphysics—that dissent inherently threatens order.

From a van Fraassen perspective, this is a misuse of theory: it exceeds the bounds of empirical adequacy and imposes metaphysical commitments that cannot be justified.

2. Voluntarism and the Ethics of Acceptance

Van Fraassen emphasizes that accepting a theory does not mean believing it is true in all respects—it means committing to its empirical adequacy. Dr. Chen’s refusal to “accept” the state’s narrative is not irrational; it is a voluntarist stance: he does not find the theory adequate to the facts, and therefore does not commit to it.

His silence is a philosophical act: a refusal to endorse a theory that lacks empirical support and demands metaphysical submission. In this light, his behavior is not defiance—it is epistemic integrity.

3. The Role of Observation and the Limits of Authority

Constructive Empiricism places observation at the center of epistemic legitimacy. Dr. Chen’s case reveals a system that punishes what cannot be observed—intentions, loyalties, ideological purity. The charges rely on unobservable constructs, not on verifiable actions.

Van Fraassen would argue that this is a category error: the state treats metaphysical conformity as if it were an observable phenomenon, and uses this confusion to justify coercion.

4. Conclusion: Against Metaphysical Coercion

From the standpoint of Bas van Fraassen’s Constructive Empiricism, the case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan is a philosophical warning. The state demands belief in a theory of political order that is neither empirically adequate nor epistemically modest. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess is a refusal to accept a metaphysical claim disguised as legal fact.

“Science does not aim at truth, but at empirical adequacy.” — The Scientific Image

Dr. Chen’s silence is not obstruction—it is a van Fraassen-style protest, a defense of the right to withhold belief when the evidence does not compel it.
