From George Edward Moore’s common sense realism, the case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan underscores the moral clarity that arises not from abstract theory or ideological distortion, but from the plain facts of human conscience, justice, and intellectual integrity. His silence is not enigmatic—it is a straightforward refusal to deny what is obviously true.

1. Common Sense as the Foundation of Moral Judgment

Moore’s philosophy insists that certain truths—such as “injustice is wrong” or “freedom is good”—are self-evident, grasped through common sense and not reducible to complex theoretical systems. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess is grounded in this kind of moral clarity. He does not need elaborate justification to know that coerced confession is wrong, and that truth should not be punished.

His silence affirms what any reasonable person can see: It is wrong to punish someone for thinking freely and speaking truthfully.

2. The Naturalistic Fallacy: Justice Is Not What Power Defines

Moore famously warned against the “naturalistic fallacy”—confusing what is good with what is socially or politically accepted. In Dr. Chen’s case, the state equates obedience with virtue and dissent with guilt. But Moore’s realism insists that goodness is not defined by authority. Dr. Chen’s silence is a rejection of this fallacy—a refusal to let power dictate moral truth.

He reminds us that justice is not what the law enforces—it is what conscience recognizes.

3. Moral Facts Are Objective and Knowable

Moore believed that moral facts are real and knowable, even if they cannot be reduced to physical properties. Dr. Chen’s case is not morally ambiguous—it is a situation where the wrongness of repression and the rightness of integrity are plainly visible. His silence is a stand for moral realism, a refusal to pretend that truth is negotiable.

He does not speak because truth is not up for debate—it is self-evident to the honest mind.

4. Clarity Over Complexity: The Ethics of Simplicity

Moore’s style of philosophy favored clarity over complexity, resisting the temptation to obscure truth with jargon or abstraction. Dr. Chen’s silence reflects this ethos. He does not engage in rhetorical games—he simply refuses to lie. His case is a reminder that moral courage often looks like quiet simplicity, not dramatic defiance.

His silence says: I will not say what is false. That is enough.

5. Conclusion: Silence as Common Sense Integrity

From George Edward Moore’s common sense realism, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s silence is a moral act of clarity. It affirms that truth is not a matter of ideology, but of plain recognition. It rejects the confusion of power with goodness, and insists that justice begins with the refusal to betray what is obviously right.

“Here is one hand, and here is another.” — G.E. Moore

Dr. Chen’s silence is such a gesture. It does not argue—it shows. It does not theorize—it stands. And in doing so, it reminds us that truth is not hidden—it is simply denied by those who fear it.
