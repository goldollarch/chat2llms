A Kelsenian Pure Theory of Law Perspective on the Chen Jingyuan Case

Hans Kelsen’s Pure Theory of Law (Reine Rechtslehre), developed in the early 20th century and refined in his 1934 and 1960 works, seeks to establish jurisprudence as a “pure” science, stripped of extraneous elements like morality, politics, sociology, or psychology. Kelsen insists that law is a normative order—a hierarchical system of “ought” statements (Sollen)—whose validity derives solely from formal efficacy and procedural derivation from a grundnorm (basic norm), the presupposed ultimate source of legal authority. Law’s “is” (Sein) and “ought” (Sollen) are rigorously separated: jurists describe the structure of valid norms, not their substantive justice or empirical effects. Violations of norms trigger sanctions, but the theory remains neutral on whether those sanctions are “good” or “bad.” In this framework, the Chen Jingyuan case—wherein a scholar was convicted in 2023 of “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) for forwarding low-engagement Twitter posts—serves as a stark illustration of normative validity’s fragility when procedural hierarchies falter, potentially undermining the grundnorm’s efficacy without impugning law’s purity.

Kelsen’s theory posits law as a dynamic, coercive order where each norm’s validity stems from a higher norm, culminating in the grundnorm—a fictional, presupposed “ought” that validates the entire pyramid (e.g., the state’s monopoly on force). In China, this grundnorm might be the Constitution’s supremacy, with the Criminal Law and procedural codes as derivative norms. Chen’s alleged offense—disseminating “false information” via posts on art (e.g., the “umbrella girl” cartoon), emotion (e.g., June 4th candlelight memorials), theory (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s communism critique, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and history (e.g., Mao’s Selected Works edits, Deng’s retirement endorsement)—falls under Article 293 of the Criminal Law, interpreted via the 2013 Supreme People’s Court and Supreme People’s Procuratorate guidelines on network defamation. The “two highs” interpretation deems such acts criminal if they “fabricate or knowingly spread false information” causing “serious public disorder,” with quantitative thresholds like 500 reposts. Yet, Chen’s account (@_cenjoy) had near-zero followers and under 100 reposts, with negligible engagement—empirically, no disorder ensued.

From Kelsen’s vantage, the case’s validity hinges not on the posts’ “truth” (a factual, non-normative question) but on whether the judicial process adhered to the normative hierarchy: indictment by the procuratorate, trial by the court, and appeal upholding. Procedurally, flaws abound: the trial was non-public (contra Criminal Procedure Law Article 11’s publicity principle), defenses were curtailed (violating Article 14’s right to counsel), and the prison complaint letter was suppressed (breaching Prison Law Article 22). The “sorting” of posts as “false” lacked formal appraisal or evidence of causation, rendering the “evidence chain” a procedural fiction. Kelsen would note that while the conviction formally derives from the grundnorm (state sovereignty), its efficacy is compromised by deviations from higher norms (e.g., presumption of innocence, Article 12). This creates a “normative gap”: the sanction (18 months’ imprisonment) is “valid” in form but lacks the procedural purity to fully validate it, echoing Kelsen’s view that law’s coercion must trace uninterruptedly upward.

Kelsen’s separation of Sein and Sollen is crucial here: the judiciary’s moralistic framing—“insulting national leaders,” “disrupting order”—intrudes empirical and ethical judgments into the normative sphere, polluting the “pure” ought. The vague “pocket crime” nature of Article 293 allows arbitrary application, where “false information” blurs into political sensitivity, violating Kelsen’s demand for clear, hierarchical norms. Chen’s defense—invoking Gödel’s incompleteness to question “knowing falsity” in complex systems—highlights this: law should prescribe “oughts” (e.g., prove intent via evidence), not import “is” facts (e.g., presumed malice from education). The selective enforcement—state media republishing similar content unscathed—further erodes validity, as equal application is a derivative norm from constitutional equality.

Yet, Kelsen’s theory remains neutral: even flawed, the conviction is “valid” if unrevoked, underscoring law’s efficacy over justice. This detachment critiques the case’s substantive absurdity without moralizing—reform requires strengthening the normative pyramid, ensuring procedural purity to bolster the grundnorm’s legitimacy. Chen’s resolute appeal and prison writings affirm individual agency within the order, but the case warns: impure norms risk systemic delegitimation, where coercion without validity breeds resistance.

In sum, Kelsen’s pure theory illuminates Chen’s case as a procedural impurity in a hierarchical edifice: formally valid yet structurally vulnerable. By refocusing on Sollen’s autonomy, Chinese jurisprudence could purify itself, aligning efficacy with the grundnorm’s promise of coherent order. Chen’s ordeal, devoid of moral verdict, stands as a normative cautionary tale.
