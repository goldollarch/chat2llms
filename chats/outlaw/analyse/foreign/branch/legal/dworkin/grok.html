A Dworkinian Critique of the Chen Jingyuan Case: Integrity, Rights as Trumps, and the Moral Reading of Law

Ronald Dworkin’s legal philosophy, as elaborated in seminal works like Taking Rights Seriously (1977) and Law’s Empire (1986), offers a robust framework for evaluating the rule of law through the lens of moral integrity and individual rights. Dworkin rejects legal positivism’s separation of law from morality, arguing instead for a “moral reading” where law is an interpretive enterprise aimed at the best constructive interpretation that fits precedent while promoting justice and fairness. Central to this is the principle of integrity: judges must treat law as a coherent narrative, not a mechanical application of rules, seeking the interpretation that best justifies the community’s practices as a matter of political morality. Rights, Dworkin insists, function as “trumps” over collective goals—personal liberties like free expression cannot be overridden by utilitarian concerns for “social order” unless the right itself demands it. In the 2023 conviction of Dr. Chen Jingyuan for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪), the Kunming judiciary’s handling starkly fails this Dworkinian test: a rushed, opaque process that sacrifices interpretive integrity for expedient suppression, subordinating constitutional rights to fabricated threats.

Chen’s case hinges on innocuous Twitter forwards from his low-profile account (@_cenjoy)—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). With near-zero followers and under 100 reposts, these elicited no measurable impact, yet Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun “sorted” them as “false information” “insulting national leaders and the political system,” causing “serious public disorder.” Dworkin would decry this as a betrayal of integrity: the judiciary ignored the best moral reading of the law, opting for a positivist “rule-book” approach that stretches the vague “pocket crime” of Article 293 of the Criminal Law to criminalize private inquiry. Integrity demands a narrative that coheres with China’s constitutional commitment to free speech (Article 35) and scientific research (Article 47), interpreting Chen’s actions as exercises of these rights rather than subversion. By presuming “high education equals malice” without evidence of intent (mens rea), the court fragments legal coherence, treating law as a blunt instrument rather than a principled empire.

Dworkin’s “rights as trumps” principle further indicts the decision: individual liberties like expression and thought are not mere policy preferences but entrenched protections against majoritarian overreach. Chen’s forwards—art’s symbolic play, emotion’s subjective voice, theory’s contestation, history’s unvarnished record—embody the right to engage diverse ideas, a cornerstone of intellectual freedom. The court’s utilitarian calculus (“disrupting order”) trumps these without justification, echoing Dworkin’s critique of “majoritarian” balancing in Taking Rights Seriously, where rights yield only to countervailing rights, not vague societal harms. With no proof of actual disorder (zero engagement, no societal ripple), the conviction inverts the hierarchy: collective “stability” overrides personal autonomy, rendering rights hollow. This selective enforcement—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—exposes procedural unfairness, violating Dworkin’s equal treatment under law.

Procedurally, the non-public trial, denied defenses, and suppressed prison letter flout Dworkin’s emphasis on adversarial integrity: justice requires open moral argument, not secretive fiat. In Law’s Empire, Dworkin likens judging to chain novels—each decision must fit and justify the ongoing story. Here, the Kunming court abandons this for a positivist edict, ignoring precedents like the 2024 Supreme People’s Court guidelines restricting “pocket crimes” to genuine threats. Chen’s Prison Blood Letter, invoking Gödel’s incompleteness to affirm inquiry’s limits, exemplifies the principled resistance Dworkin champions: a call for law’s moral renewal.

Dworkin’s philosophy thus condemns Chen’s case as a failure of interpretive justice, where rights are demoted and integrity sacrificed. Yet, it also offers hope: true rule of law demands Hercules-like judges who craft the best moral narrative, protecting expression as a trump for human dignity. Reopening Chen’s case would reaffirm this, aligning China’s legal empire with its constitutional ideals and fostering the principled community Dworkin envisions. In an era of global scrutiny, such reform is not just moral—it’s imperative for legitimacy.
