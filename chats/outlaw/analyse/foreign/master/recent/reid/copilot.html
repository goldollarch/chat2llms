From the perspective of Thomas Reid and the Scottish School of Common Sense, the case of Dr. Chen Jingyuan exemplifies the moral clarity and epistemic integrity that arise from our natural faculties of reason and conscience. His silence is not a mystery to be decoded by ideological speculation—it is a plain and principled act, intelligible to any honest observer who trusts the basic capacities of human understanding.

1. Common Sense as the Foundation of Moral Judgment

Reid argued that human beings are endowed with innate faculties—perception, memory, moral sense—that allow us to grasp self-evident truths. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess is not an enigma requiring theoretical deconstruction; it is a clear moral signal, accessible to any person who has not been blinded by fear or propaganda.

His silence says plainly: I will not affirm what I know to be false, nor deny what I know to be just.

2. The Trustworthiness of Human Faculties

Reid rejected the skepticism of thinkers like Hume, insisting that our senses, memory, and conscience are fundamentally reliable. In this light, Dr. Chen’s case does not require elaborate justification to be understood as unjust. The ordinary person, using their natural faculties, can see that coercing a scholar into silence or confession violates basic principles of justice and truth.

His silence appeals not to elite theory, but to the shared moral intuitions of reasonable people.

3. Language and the Abuse of Abstraction

Reid was wary of philosophical systems that obscured truth through abstraction. Similarly, the state’s use of vague charges—“picking quarrels,” “provoking trouble”—is a linguistic obfuscation, designed to mask the absence of real wrongdoing. Dr. Chen’s silence is a rejection of this misuse of language, a demand that words correspond to facts, not serve as tools of repression.

He reminds us that truth must be spoken plainly, not buried in euphemism or ideological fog.

4. Moral Courage as a Common Virtue

For Reid, moral principles are not the domain of philosophers alone—they are accessible to all rational beings. Dr. Chen’s silence is not heroic in a romantic sense; it is ordinary moral courage, the kind that arises when a person refuses to betray their conscience. It is the kind of virtue that Reid believed was woven into the fabric of human nature.

His resistance is not esoteric—it is common sense fidelity to what is right.

5. Conclusion: Silence as the Voice of Reasonable Conscience

From the Scottish School of Common Sense, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s silence is a transparent moral act, grounded in the trustworthiness of human reason and conscience. It rejects the manipulation of language, affirms the clarity of ethical perception, and calls upon others to see what is plainly before them: that truth does not require confession, and justice does not require submission.

“The evidence of sense is the foundation of all reasoning.” — Thomas Reid

Dr. Chen’s silence is such evidence. It does not need to be decoded—it needs only to be recognized by those who have not abandoned their common sense.
