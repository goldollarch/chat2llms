Rousseauian Reflections on the Chen Jingyuan Case: The Social Contract Betrayed and the Will of the People Suppressed

Jean-Jacques Rousseau’s political philosophy, most profoundly articulated in The Social Contract (1762) and Discourse on Inequality (1755), envisions a society where individuals surrender their natural liberties to form a collective body governed by the general will (volonté générale)—the sovereign expression of the people’s common interest. This contract restores the freedom lost in unequal civil society, ensuring equality under law and preventing arbitrary power. Rousseau warns that when rulers or institutions deviate from the general will, they become despotic, corrupting the polity and alienating citizens from their sovereign essence. As he declares, “The strongest is never strong enough to be always master, unless he transforms strength into right, and obedience into duty.” In this light, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪)—a vague charge for forwarding innocuous Twitter posts—exposes a profound betrayal of the social contract: a judiciary, ostensibly serving the people, instead enforces private interests over the general will, stifling the very discourse essential to sovereign deliberation.

Rousseau’s ideal polity hinges on the general will emerging from free, informed citizens engaging in transparent debate, untainted by factionalism or coercion. Chen, an independent scholar from a humble Yunnan background, embodies this citizen-sovereign: his Ph.D. in complex systems physics reflects a life of inquiry, not agitation. In 2022, while briefly in Kunming, he was arrested for sharing posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy)—artistic symbols like the “umbrella girl” cartoon (evoking resilience), emotional tributes such as June 4th candlelight images, theoretical reflections (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). With near-zero followers and under 100 reposts, these acts caused no measurable disorder—mere whispers in the digital ether. Yet Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun “sorted” them as “false information” insulting leaders and disrupting “public order,” imposing a one-year-eight-month sentence without public trial or full defense. This procedural opacity and selective enforcement (state media republished similar content unscathed) violate Rousseau’s dictum: laws must reflect the general will, not the whims of magistrates. As Rousseau asserts, “The general will is always right and tends to the public advantage,” but when “particular wills” dominate, the contract dissolves into tyranny.

Chen’s case starkly illustrates Rousseau’s critique of corrupted civil society, where inequality breeds alienation. In the state of nature, humans are free and equal; civilization introduces artificial hierarchies that Rousseau decries as “chains” forged by the powerful. The judiciary’s presumption—“high education implies knowledge of falsity”—inverts this: Chen’s scholarly essence, a tool for mutual enlightenment, becomes evidence of subversion, reducing him to an “object” of state suspicion. Forwarding global ideas—art’s symbolism, emotion’s authenticity, theory’s contestation, history’s lessons—fosters the informed populace Rousseau deemed vital for the general will. Yet, by criminalizing them under a “pocket crime,” the authorities prioritize “particular wills” (e.g., protecting elite narratives) over collective sovereignty, echoing Rousseau’s warning in Discourse on Political Economy: “The people must be enlightened… to prevent the abuse of power.” Selective enforcement—sparing state outlets like Guangming Daily—exposes the contract’s fracture: equality is illusory when the weak (Chen, with his negligible reach) bear the lash while the strong evade it.

Rousseau’s solution lies in sovereign renewal: the people, as ultimate arbiters, must reclaim the general will through vigilance and education. Chen’s Prison Blood Letter—a defiant manifesto vowing “life without end, struggle without cease” and lifelong accountability for his accusers—resonates as such renewal. From iron bars, he invokes Gödel’s limits to affirm intellectual humility, exposing procedural absurdities (no evidence, suppressed appeals) as breaches of the contract. This is Rousseauian citizenship: not passive obedience, but active guardianship of the common good. As he notes, “Every citizen… must be convinced that what decides the fate of all should be decided by all.” Chen’s case, then, is a clarion call: in an era of “comprehensive rule of law,” deviations like non-public trials and fabricated “disorder” erode the general will, risking the despotism Rousseau feared.

Ultimately, viewed through Rousseau, Chen’s ordeal underscores the fragility of the social contract: when justice serves particular interests, it alienates the sovereign people from their freedom. Reaffirming equality—through transparent trials, evidence-based verdicts, and protections for discourse—would honor the contract’s promise. Chen’s unyielding pursuit of truth, even in chains, exemplifies the vigilant citizen Rousseau championed: a reminder that true sovereignty demands not silence, but the bold articulation of the general will. In reclaiming this, China can transcend inequality’s shadows, forging a polity where liberty and equality endure.
