Grok

David Hume’s philosophy, as expounded in A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–1740) and An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748), anchors knowledge in sensory experience while wielding skepticism as a scalpel against unexamined certainties. Central to his empiricism is the distinction between “impressions” (vivid sensory data) and “ideas” (fainter copies thereof), with all meaningful ideas traceable to impressions; without this grounding, concepts dissolve into fancy. His skepticism targets causal inference and induction: we observe constant conjunctions (e.g., billiard balls colliding) but cannot rationally prove necessity, rendering claims of hidden causes (e.g., “this post will cause disorder”) mere habitual associations, vulnerable to the problem of induction. Hume’s “fork” bifurcates knowledge into “relations of ideas” (analytic truths, like mathematics, certain but trivial) and “matters of fact” (synthetic truths, empirical but probabilistically uncertain). Miracles, self-identity, and unobservable essences fare poorly under this scrutiny, as do dogmatic assertions lacking experiential warrant. Applying Hume to Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪)—stemming from forwarding low-engagement Twitter posts—exposes the case as a triumph of ungrounded conjecture over empirical rigor, validating Chen’s skeptical self-defense while indicting the judiciary’s presumptive overreach.

Humean empiricism first undermines the prosecution’s core claim: that Chen’s forwards disseminated “false information” with “intent” (mingzhi, or knowing dissemination), causing “serious public disorder.” The posts—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” evoking symbolic resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical snippets (e.g., Mao’s Selected Works edits, Deng’s retirement endorsement)—originate from observable impressions: public domains, verifiable facts, or subjective expressions. Yet, the judiciary’s “sorting” (shūlǐ) as “insults to national leaders and the political system” lacks any impression-derived evidence—no sensory data of falsity (e.g., official debunkings), no impressions of intent (e.g., Chen’s private musings), and no observable disorder (his account’s near-zero followers and under 100 reposts yielded negligible engagement). Hume would dismiss this as idea without impression: the “falsity” is a pale, ungrounded idea, not a vivid sensory fact. Chen’s defense, invoking Gödel’s incompleteness and Humean skepticism of full knowledge, aligns seamlessly—admitting impressions’ limits (“I cannot ‘know’ falsehood in a complex world”) grounds his actions in empirical humility, not dogmatic certainty.

Skepticism further eviscerates the causal chain: the alleged “serious disorder” from “knowing” dissemination. Hume’s critique of causation as non-necessary constant conjunction applies: we observe Chen’s forwards (a conjunction of act and low impact) but cannot infer an unseen necessity linking them to societal chaos. The judiciary’s presumption—high education implies “should know” falsity, thus intent—exemplifies the problem of induction: extrapolating from past “disorder” cases to Chen’s whispers is an unreliable habit, not rational proof. No impressions support the outcome (no riots, no metrics like 500+ reposts per “two highs” guidelines); it’s a synthetic matter of fact, probabilistically frail. Chen’s SOC (self-organized criticality) rebuttal echoes this: without criticality (e.g., viral thresholds), his “perturbation” yields no avalanche—pure Humean caution against overreaching causation from sparse data.

Hume’s fork seals the evaluation: the charges blend trivial relations of ideas (e.g., “high education = discernment,” analytic but irrelevant without facts) with dubious matters of fact (e.g., “disorder ensued,” ungrounded). Procedural lapses—non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison letter—compound this, as Hume’s empiricism demands experiential transparency for any claim to knowledge. Selective enforcement (state media unscathed) smacks of the “miracle” Hume debunks: improbable without prior bias, a violation of uniform experience.

Overall, Hume would commend Chen’s defense as exemplary empiricism: humble, impression-bound, and skeptical of causal fictions, transforming personal ordeal into a critique of dogmatic authority. The case fares poorly under Humean scrutiny—lacking sensory warrant, it exemplifies how unexamined ideas masquerade as facts, perpetuating injustice. In a world of probabilistic truths, Chen’s resolute inquiry shines as rational virtue, urging a judiciary grounded in experience over presumption.
