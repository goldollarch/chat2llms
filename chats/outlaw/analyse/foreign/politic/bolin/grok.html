A Commentary on Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s Case Through the Lens of Isaiah Berlin’s Political Philosophy

Isaiah Berlin, one of the 20th century’s most influential political philosophers, offered a profound critique of monism—the belief in a single, overriding truth or value—and championed pluralism, arguing that human values are irreducibly diverse, often incommensurable, and prone to conflict. In his seminal essay “Two Concepts of Liberty” (1958), Berlin distinguished between negative liberty (freedom from interference, allowing individuals to pursue their own ends without external coercion) and positive liberty (freedom to realize one’s true self, which can devolve into authoritarianism when elites impose a “rational” vision of the good life). He warned that the pursuit of absolute harmony among values could lead to tyranny, as seen in totalitarian regimes that sacrifice diversity for a singular ideal. Berlin’s “hedgehog and fox” metaphor—from Archilochus: “The fox knows many things, but the hedgehog knows one big thing”—further underscores his preference for the fox’s pluralistic agility over the hedgehog’s monistic rigidity. Drawing on these ideas, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) in China exemplifies the perils of value monism, the erosion of negative liberty, and the suppression of pluralism in a system that prioritizes a hegemonic “order” over individual expression.

At its core, Chen’s case reveals a clash between pluralistic inquiry and monistic control, echoing Berlin’s fear that “the belief in a single true solution” can justify coercion. Chen, an independent scholar with a Ph.D. in physics specializing in complex systems, was arrested for forwarding posts on his low-profile Twitter account (@_cenjoy). These included artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing resilience in protests), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images evoking personal grief), theoretical debates (e.g., analyses of political spectrums, evaluations of Xi Jinping, Trump’s critique of communism, and Pompeo’s remarks on U.S.-China relations), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao Zedong’s Selected Works, endorsements of Deng Xiaoping’s retirement, and Sino-Ukrainian military cooperation). With zero followers and under 100 reposts, these posts generated no measurable impact—yet the judiciary “sorted” them as “false information” insulting leaders and causing “serious public disorder.” This reflects Berlin’s critique of positive liberty gone awry: the state, acting as the “rational self” of society, imposes a singular value—“harmonious order”—suppressing diverse expressions deemed threats. Berlin warned that positive liberty can become “the liberty of the wise imposed on the foolish,” leading to “despotism in the name of self-realization.” Here, Chen’s eclectic forwards—blending art, emotion, theory, and history—embody value pluralism: no single “truth” dominates; instead, they invite dialogue across incommensurable perspectives (e.g., Western critiques of communism alongside Chinese historical reflections). The conviction, however, enforces monism, reducing pluralism to “disorder” and justifying interference.

Berlin’s negative liberty—freedom from arbitrary coercion—is flagrantly violated in the procedural absurdities of Chen’s trial. Negative liberty demands protection from external barriers to self-directed action; Chen’s scholarly curation of global ideas was a private pursuit, causing no harm (as evidenced by zero societal ripple). Yet, Judge Pu Huijun’s presumption that Chen’s “high education” implied he “should know” the posts were false twists this into culpability, echoing Berlin’s concern that monistic regimes redefine freedom as conformity to a “higher” rational order. The non-public trial, denial of full defense, suppressed prison complaints, and selective enforcement (state media republished similar content unscathed) exemplify this: the “they” of bureaucratic authority—rigid, hedgehog-like in its single-minded “national security” narrative—erodes the fox’s agile multiplicity. Berlin, drawing from Kant and Mill, argued that “to manipulate men, to propel them toward goals which you—the social reformer—see, but they may not, is to deny their human essence.” Chen’s case illustrates this denial: his posts, far from monolithic propaganda, reflect the incommensurability of values—artistic freedom vs. state harmony, emotional truth vs. official narrative—yet the judiciary, fearing conflict, imposes a false unity through punishment.

The case also highlights Berlin’s pluralism as a safeguard against totalitarianism. Values like free expression, intellectual inquiry, and historical truth are not hierarchically reconcilable with “social order”; they clash, and pluralism demands tolerance of this agony. Berlin wrote: “The notion of the perfect whole…must remain an ideal; but an ideal which no one can realize without entering into conflict with other values.” In convicting Chen without evidence of harm (no quantifiable disorder), the system privileges a monistic “harmony,” sacrificing pluralism’s vitality. This risks what Berlin called the “slippery slope” to oppression: starting with “picking quarrels,” it could engulf all dissent, as seen in the case’s irony—posts echoing global debates (e.g., critiques of communism) are deemed threats, yet Berlin himself navigated such pluralism in Cold War liberalism.

Ultimately, Berlin’s philosophy urges a revaluation: justice thrives not in monistic certainty but in pluralistic humility. Chen’s resilience—his Prison Blood Letter vowing lifelong accountability for his accusers—embodies the fox’s cunning: navigating incommensurables without surrender. In a Berlinian view, reforming such systems requires embracing value conflicts, protecting negative liberty, and rejecting the hedgehog’s tyranny. Chen’s case warns: without pluralism, “rule of law” becomes a monistic cage, stifling the human spirit’s diverse pursuits. To heed Berlin: let a thousand values bloom, lest one devours all.
