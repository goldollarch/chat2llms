A Structuralist Commentary on Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s Case: The Semiotics of Judicial Signification and the Binary of Order/Disorder

Structuralism, as a philosophical paradigm pioneered by Ferdinand de Saussure in linguistics and extended by thinkers like Claude Lévi-Strauss in anthropology and Roland Barthes in semiotics, posits that human phenomena—language, culture, and social institutions—are governed by underlying structures of signs and relations, rather than isolated events or individual intentions. Meaning emerges not from inherent essences but from differential systems: binary oppositions (e.g., true/false, sacred/profane) organize the signifying chain, where the value of one term derives from its opposition to another. Deep structures (structures profondes) underlie surface manifestations (structures superficielles), rendering individual actions intelligible only within the relational network. In Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪)—stemming from forwarding low-impact Twitter posts—the case exemplifies how judicial discourse functions as a semiotic apparatus, imposing a rigid binary of “order/disorder” to suppress polysemy and enforce a monolithic state narrative. Through this lens, Chen’s innocuous shares are not crimes but disruptions to the signifying structure, revealing the law’s role in perpetuating cultural hegemony.

At the surface level, Chen’s actions appear innocuous: on his account (@_cenjoy), with zero followers and under 100 reposts, he shared artifacts of cultural multiplicity—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” evoking symbolic resistance), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical documents (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). These are not declarative statements but fragments of a signifying web: the cartoon’s polysemy (resilience vs. subversion), the memorial’s affective relationality (grief as social bond), the theories’ oppositional play (ideological difference as generative tension), and the histories’ archival traces (past as differential marker). Saussurean semiotics would view them as signifiers in a chain, deriving value from absence and relation—e.g., Trump’s critique gains meaning against communist orthodoxy, not as inherent “falsity.” Yet, the Kunming judiciary—Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun—“sorts” (梳理) these into a binary opposition: “true information” (state-aligned order) vs. “false rumors” (disruptive chaos). This is the structuralist moment of reduction: surface diversity is subordinated to deep structure, where the law’s langue (system of signs) privileges the signified of “national security,” erasing relational nuance.

This binary is no neutral taxonomy but a power-laden myth, per Barthes’ extension of structuralism in Mythologies (1957): judicial discourse naturalizes ideology, turning contingency into essence. The presumption of Chen’s “high education = knowledge of falsity” exemplifies this: education, a signifier of enlightenment, is inverted to signify malice, collapsing the opposition into a tautology (educated = subversive). With no evidence of impact (zero engagement, no quantifiable disorder), the “serious public disruption” charge is a floating signifier, anchored retroactively to the binary of loyalty/treason. Lévi-Strauss’s anthropological insight applies: like kinship structures mediating raw/cooked oppositions, the law here mediates “order/disorder” to resolve cultural tensions—Chen’s globalist shares (art from Hong Kong protests, Western critiques) threaten the deep structure of national unity, so they are “cooked” into criminality. Procedural opacity—non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison letter—further entrenches this: the signifying process is hidden, mimicking the unconscious grammar Saussure described, where rules generate without revelation.

Yet, structuralism’s radical potential lies in exposing these systems for subversion. Chen’s Prison Blood Letter disrupts the binary: invoking Gödel’s incompleteness, he reveals truth’s relational undecidability, fracturing “true/false.” His classification of posts (art as non-factual, emotion as subjective, theory as dialogic, history as verifiable) remaps the signifying field, introducing third terms that evade opposition—e.g., “mutual learning” (文明互鉴) over “attack.” Selective enforcement (state media unscathed) lays bare the structure’s asymmetry: the binary serves power, not universality. In Saussurean terms, Chen’s defense is a parole (speech act) challenging the langue (legal code), hinting at multiplicity beyond the system.

Ultimately, Chen’s case underscores structuralism’s double bind: while deep structures enable meaning, they constrain it, turning inquiry into threat. The judiciary’s myth of “order” is a cultural code preserving hegemony, but Chen’s resilient re-signification—vowing eternal struggle—suggests the possibility of bricolage: piecing fragments into new myths. In a world of differential signs, his ordeal warns: without interrogating binaries, justice remains a closed system, echoing Lévi-Strauss’s lament for lost mediations. Chen’s quiet revolt invites us to decode the code, lest the structure calcify into silence.
