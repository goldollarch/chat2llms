Grok

Epicurus (341–270 BCE), the founder of Epicureanism, posited pleasure (hēdonē) as the highest good, but not in the vulgar sense of excess—rather, as the absence of pain (aponia) in the body and disturbance (ataraxia) in the soul. In his Letter to Menoeceus, he argues that true happiness arises from prudent choices: cultivating simple desires (for food, shelter, friendship), shunning vain pursuits (wealth, fame, politics), and dispelling unfounded fears (of gods, death, or fate). The universe, per his atomistic materialism, is a random swirl of atoms in the void, indifferent to human affairs; gods exist but are unconcerned, and death is mere dissolution—“when we exist, death is not; when death exists, we do not.” Society’s role, via natural justice and social contract, is to safeguard mutual security, not impose arbitrary suffering. Laws should promote tranquility, punishing only what harms others, as “justice is a pledge of reciprocal utility.” Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪)—for forwarding low-impact Twitter posts—violates these tenets, transforming a scholar’s quest for serene inquiry into a vortex of needless pain, while exposing the judiciary’s failure to uphold Epicurean equity.

Chen’s actions align beautifully with Epicurean prudence: as an independent scholar of complex systems, his forwards on his negligible account (@_cenjoy)—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” evoking quiet resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images as cathartic reflection), theoretical exchanges (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical notes (e.g., Mao’s Selected Works edits, Deng’s retirement endorsement)—were modest pursuits of intellectual tranquility. With zero followers and under 100 reposts, they caused no harm, no “serious public disorder,” embodying Epicurus’s call for “calculated hedonism”: selecting natural, necessary desires (knowledge as a stable pleasure) while avoiding the turbulent seas of politics. Chen’s atomistic worldview—acknowledging cognitive limits via Gödel’s incompleteness—mirrors Epicurus’s materialist calm: in a void of uncertain truths, one seeks ataraxia through humble inquiry, not dogmatic certainty. His low-key engagement shunned the “empty opinions” of fame, fostering the soul’s peace through friendship with ideas, much like Epicurus’s Garden, a haven from Athenian strife.

The Kunming judiciary—Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun—perverts this ethos, inflicting gratuitous suffering in bad faith. Epicurean justice demands reciprocity: laws as pacts to prevent mutual injury, not tools for capricious pain. Yet, the “pocket crime” charge fabricates harm from nothingness—no evidence of impact, no causal link to “disorder,” just a presumption of “high education = malice” in “sorting” innocuous posts as “insults” or “rumors.” This echoes Epicurus’s critique of superstitious fears: the court’s terror of “subversion” (fear of gods reimagined as ideological phantoms) breeds irrational punishment, disturbing the souls of innocents like Chen. Procedural injustices—non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison letter—violate the social contract, imposing ataraxia’s opposite: anxiety and alienation. Selective enforcement (state media republishing similar content unscathed) reveals utility’s inversion: laws serve the powerful, not the common good, fostering the vain pleasures of control over prudent equity.

Chen’s response, however, radiates Epicurean fortitude: from his Prison Blood Letter, vowing “life without end, struggle without cease” and lifelong accountability, he chooses calculated resilience—confronting pain without resentment, affirming simple joys (truth-seeking, scholarly essence) amid turmoil. Like Epicurus facing Epicurean calumnies, Chen dispels judicial “fears” (of chaos from whispers) with lucid reason: his forwards as harmless atoms in the void, incapable of harm. His unyielding pursuit—eternal recurrence of inquiry despite bars—embodies ataraxia: inner calm from virtue, scorning death’s shadow (imprisonment as temporary dissolution). In a world of indifferent atoms (judicial indifference), he forges utility through critique, urging mutual security via open discourse.

Epicureanism thus condemns Chen’s case as a tragic inversion: a prudent life disrupted by vain fears, a contract broken by one-sided utility. It calls for restoration—judicial restraint, evidence-based equity—to reclaim tranquility. Chen’s defiant serenity reminds us: in the garden of reason, even thorns yield prudent blooms. Justice, per Epicurus, is not vengeance but the quiet pact of free souls.
