A Post-Structuralist Commentary on Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s Case: Deconstructing the Signifiers of Subversion

Post-structuralism, with its relentless interrogation of stable meanings, power-laden discourses, and the illusory fixity of truth, offers a potent lens for unraveling the Chen Jingyuan case—a 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) that exposes the judiciary as a discursive apparatus fabricating “crime” from the debris of signification. Drawing on Jacques Derrida’s deconstruction, Michel Foucault’s archaeology of knowledge/power, and Jean Baudrillard’s simulacra, the case reveals not a coherent “legal” narrative but a web of deferred meanings, where innocuous Twitter forwards are hyperbolized into threats to “social order.” Dr. Chen, an independent scholar with a Ph.D. in physics specializing in complex systems, forwarded posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy)—artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing resistance), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical snippets (e.g., Mao’s Selected Works edits, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement)—totaling under 100 reposts with zero followers and negligible engagement. Yet, Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun “sorted” these as “false information” insulting leaders and causing “serious public disorder,” a process that post-structuralism unmasks as the violent imposition of binary hierarchies: truth/falsehood, loyalty/subversion, order/chaos.

Derrida’s deconstruction illuminates the case’s core instability: signification is never self-present but endlessly deferred through différance—the play of differences that undermines fixed origins. The judiciary’s “sorting” (梳理) of Chen’s posts privileges a state-centric binary, reducing polysemic signs to monolithic threats. The “umbrella girl” cartoon, a floating signifier evoking resilience or critique depending on the interpretive chain, is chained to “insult”—a hierarchical violence that suppresses its traces of cultural mutuality or emotional resonance. Similarly, theoretical posts like Trump’s communism critique or Pompeo’s remarks are not neutral “facts” but nodes in a differential network of global discourse; labeling them “attacks” erases their relational play, enforcing a logocentric “truth” where the state’s voice is originary. Chen’s defense, invoking Gödel’s incompleteness theorem, echoes Derrida: no absolute “knowledge of falsity” exists in a system of undecidables—meaning is always under erasure (sous rature). The procedural farce—non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison letter—further deconstructs justice: the “evidence chain” is a simulacrum of closure, deferring accountability in an endless chain of signifiers.

Foucault’s post-structuralist genealogy of power/knowledge extends this: the law is not a neutral arbiter but a discursive formation producing “truth” to normalize power relations. Chen’s case exemplifies the judiciary as a “disciplinary apparatus,” where “picking quarrels”—a vague “pocket crime”—functions as a regime of truth, classifying intellectual curiosity as pathology. The presumption that “high education” implies “malice” is a biopolitical inscription: knowledge becomes a dangerous excess, regulated to fit the state’s epistemic grid. Selective enforcement—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—reveals the “regime of truth” as class-inflected: elite discourses are exempt, while Chen’s “small narrative” is pathologized. Foucault’s Discipline and Punish applies: the shift from spectacle to surveillance manifests in digital “sorting,” turning private forwards into panoptic threats. The absurd “serious disorder” from zero-impact posts underscores the discursive production of danger—power doesn’t repress; it incites subjects to self-police through fear of the Other’s gaze.

Baudrillard’s simulacra adds a hyperreal twist: the “crime” is a third-order simulation, where signs of “disorder” precede and supplant any reality. With no tangible harm (engagement near zero), the judicial narrative fabricates a hyperreal threat—a “desert of the real” where posts are not events but models of subversion, circulating in a closed loop of state media and court documents. Chen’s low-profile account, a whisper in the digital void, becomes a spectacular “avalanche” through simulation, echoing Baudrillard’s Gulf War: “it didn’t take place,” yet the spectacle demands punishment.

Post-structuralism thus deconstructs Chen’s case as a discursive violence: meanings deferred, power inscribed, reality simulated. Yet, in this fragmentation lies resistance—Chen’s Prison Blood Letter, with its Gödelian humility and vow of “lifelong struggle,” disrupts the binary by affirming undecidability. In a world of endless sign-play, his inquiry persists as a trace, subverting the totalizing gaze. The case warns: without dismantling these hierarchies, justice remains a hollow signifier, echoing post-structuralism’s ironic call: the end of metanarratives is not liberation, but the birth of vigilant, unending critique.
