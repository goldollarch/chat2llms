An Analysis of Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s Case from John Stuart Mill’s Philosophical Perspective

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), a foundational figure in liberalism and utilitarianism, provides a robust framework for examining cases involving freedom of expression and state intervention. In his seminal work On Liberty (1859), Mill advances the “harm principle” as the sole legitimate basis for restricting individual liberty: “the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others.” This principle stems from his utilitarian ethic, which seeks the “greatest happiness for the greatest number,” where happiness encompasses intellectual, moral, and practical development. Mill ardently defends freedom of speech, arguing that suppressing opinions—even false ones—harms society by stifling the “marketplace of ideas,” where truth emerges through open debate and refutation. He warns that censorship not only risks silencing truth but also enfeebles intellectual vitality, as unchallenged doctrines become “dead dogmas.” From this vantage, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪) in China represents a profound violation of Mill’s principles: a case where harmless expression is criminalized, diminishing societal utility and progress.

The Harm Principle and the Absence of Harm in Chen’s Actions

Mill’s harm principle draws a clear line: individual actions are sovereign unless they directly harm others’ rights or well-being. In Chen’s case, the prosecution claimed his Twitter forwards—on a zero-follower account with under 100 reposts—disseminated “false information” insulting leaders and causing “serious public disorder.” The posts included artistic cartoons (e.g., the “umbrella girl” symbolizing protest resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., Mao’s Selected Works edits, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement). Mill would interrogate: where is the harm? These expressions inflicted no physical, economic, or verifiable injury—engagement was negligible, with no evidence of societal disruption (e.g., riots, panic, or measurable unrest). Absent harm, state intervention is tyrannical, as Mill argues: “Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.”

The judiciary’s “high education implies knowledge of falsity” presumption further offends Mill: it punishes thought and intent without overt harm, echoing his critique of “social tyranny” where majority opinion suppresses minorities without justification. From a utilitarian view, this harms the greater good: suppressing Chen’s scholarly inquiries (e.g., complex systems analysis applied to social dynamics) deprives society of intellectual growth, reducing overall happiness by fostering conformity over innovation.

Freedom of Speech as the Marketplace of Ideas: Suppression’s Utility Costs

Mill’s strongest defense of speech liberty lies in its epistemic utility: truth is forged in the crucible of debate, and even erroneous opinions serve progress by sharpening refutations or revealing partial truths. “If the opinion is right, [people] are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth: if wrong, they lose… the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error.” Chen’s posts embody this marketplace: artistic and emotional content stimulates cultural reflection, theoretical debates (e.g., critiques of communism) invite ideological contestation, and historical facts promote informed discourse. By labeling them “rumors” without evidence or open trial, the court—Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun—acts as a censor, depriving society of utility. Mill warns that such suppression enfeebles minds: unchallenged doctrines become prejudices, hindering moral and intellectual advancement.

Utilitarily, the conviction decreases happiness: Chen’s imprisonment inflicts personal suffering without societal benefit (no disorder prevented), while procedural flaws (non-public hearings, denied defenses, suppressed complaints) erode public trust, fostering fear over free inquiry. Selective enforcement—state media republished similar content unscathed—exemplifies Mill’s fear of tyrannical majorities, where utility is skewed toward the powerful, not the greatest number.

Individual Development and Social Progress: The Broader Harm

Mill extends utilitarianism beyond mere pleasure to “higher pleasures” of intellect and morality, achieved through personal liberty and diversity. Chen’s case stifles this: as a scholar, his forwards were experiments in thought, fostering self-development. Suppressing them harms societal utility by limiting “experiments in living,” Mill’s term for diverse lifestyles advancing progress. In a globalized world, such censorship isolates China from the “marketplace,” reducing collective happiness through intellectual stagnation.

Conclusion: Mill’s Verdict and Implications

From Mill’s view, Chen’s conviction fails the harm principle—no demonstrable injury justifies intervention—and undermines utilitarian progress by silencing debate. It exemplifies the “tyranny of the majority,” where state power suppresses minorities without utility gain. Revaluing the case through Mill urges decriminalizing harmless speech to maximize happiness via open inquiry. Chen’s resilience—vowing lifelong accountability—embodies Mill’s hope: free minds yield greater good. In sum, the case harms society’s intellectual utility, calling for reform to align with liberal utilitarianism’s emancipatory vision.
