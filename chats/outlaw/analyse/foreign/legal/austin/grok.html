A Comment on the Chen Jingyuan Case: An Analytical Jurisprudential Perspective from John Austin

John Austin’s analytical jurisprudence, as expounded in his seminal The Province of Jurisprudence Determined (1832), offers a stark, positivist framework for dissecting the nature of law: it is nothing more than the command of a sovereign, backed by the threat of sanction. Law, in Austin’s view, is a species of command—general precepts issued by a political superior (the sovereign) to inferiors, whose habitual obedience stems from the sovereign’s monopoly on effective coercion. Positive law (the “really existing” law) is divorced from morality, natural rights, or divine will; it is a system of imperatives, enforceable through penalties, and distinct from mere customs or ethical norms. The sovereign is that person or body to whom obedience is habitually rendered, not out of moral duty but practical fear of reprisal. This command theory strips law to its coercive essence, emphasizing its “imperative” character over substantive justice. Applied to Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s 2023 conviction for “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” (寻衅滋事罪), Austin’s lens reveals the case not as a moral failing but as a textbook exercise in sovereign command—arbitrary, sanction-backed, and devoid of intrinsic legitimacy—exposing the fragility of positive law when divorced from rational limits.

In Austinian terms, Chen’s prosecution exemplifies the sovereign’s untrammeled command: the Kunming judiciary, as an arm of the state sovereign (the central authority whose edicts are habitually obeyed), issued a general precept via the vague “pocket crime” of Article 293 of the Criminal Law, interpreting his Twitter forwards as “false information” disrupting “public order.” Chen, a physicist and independent scholar, shared low-impact posts on his account (@_cenjoy)—artistic cartoons like the “umbrella girl” (symbolizing resistance), emotional memorials such as June 4th candlelight images, theoretical discussions (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump’s critique of communism, Pompeo’s U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao’s Selected Works, Deng Xiaoping’s retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). With zero followers and under 100 reposts—yielding no measurable engagement—these acts lacked any “inferior” disruption warranting sanction. Yet, Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun enforced the command: a one-year-eight-month sentence, backed by the sovereign’s coercive apparatus (detention, non-public trial, suppressed appeals). Austin would approve the form—habitual obedience to the judiciary as sovereign delegate ensures efficacy—but decry its analytical poverty: no clear “command” violation exists, as the law’s generality (a “pocket” for any perceived threat) renders it a blunt instrument, not a precise imperative.

Austin’s insistence on sanction as law’s sine qua non illuminates the case’s coercive core. The “serious public disorder” claim, unsubstantiated by evidence (no impact data, no causation), serves as the implicit threat: obey the state’s interpretive monopoly or face imprisonment. Chen’s “high education” presumption—implying he “should know” falsehood—mirrors Austin’s superior-inferior dynamic, but absurdly: education elevates him to “inferior” status, subject to the sovereign’s whim. Procedural irregularities—denied defenses, rejected prison complaint, selective enforcement (state media republished similar content unscathed)—expose the command’s arbitrariness: law as raw power, not reasoned precept. Austin distinguishes positive law from “positive morality” (customs without sanction); here, the judiciary conflates the two, enforcing a moralistic “order” as legal command, blurring Austin’s sharp divide. This risks eroding habitual obedience: if commands appear capricious, the sovereign’s monopoly frays, as seen in Chen’s resolute Prison Blood Letter, vowing “lifelong struggle” against his accusers—a micro-rebellion against the imperative.

Austin’s positivism, while illuminating law’s coercive reality, offers scant remedy for substantive injustice, viewing morality as extraneous. Yet, Chen’s case tests this: a scholar’s inquiry—art, emotion, theory, history—branded subversive without sanction-worthy harm, reveals positive law’s potential tyranny when untethered from reason. In Austin’s framework, reform lies in clarifying commands (e.g., narrowing “pocket crimes” via evidence thresholds) to bolster sovereign efficacy. Chen’s ordeal, though, transcends positivism: his unyielding pursuit of truth amid absurdity echoes a deeper human imperative, reminding us that while law is command, justice demands more than sanction—it demands commands worthy of obedience. The case, thus, not only validates Austin’s dissection but indicts its limits: in the sovereign’s shadow, the scholar’s voice persists, a testament to law’s human frailty.
