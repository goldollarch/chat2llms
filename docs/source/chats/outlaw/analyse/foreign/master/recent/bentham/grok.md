A Utilitarian Critique of the Chen Jingyuan Case: 
Bentham's Calculus of Happiness and the Misapplication of Law

Jeremy Bentham's utilitarianism, as expounded in works like *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), posits that the moral and legal foundation of society lies in the "greatest happiness principle": actions, laws, and institutions are right insofar as they promote happiness—defined as pleasure minus pain—for the greatest number. Bentham's philosophy demands a rigorous calculus of utilities: weighing the intensity, duration, certainty, and extent of pleasures and pains to guide policy. In the realm of law and politics, this translates to a preventive, transparent system where punishment serves deterrence without excess cruelty, as in his Panopticon prison design—a structure ensuring constant visibility to maximize reformative efficiency while minimizing societal cost. Bentham rejected natural rights as metaphysical fiction, viewing law as an artificial construct to maximize collective welfare through equality, publicity, and empirical assessment. Against this backdrop, Dr. Chen Jingyuan's 2023 conviction for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (寻衅滋事罪) in Kunming emerges as a stark failure of utilitarian governance: a low-impact act of scholarly sharing yields disproportionate pain, yielding no discernible pleasure or security for society, and undermining the very happiness calculus Bentham envisioned.

Chen's case, at its core, involves the forwarding of innocuous Twitter posts on a negligible account (@_cenjoy)—artistic symbols like the "umbrella girl" cartoon evoking resilience, emotional tributes such as June 4th candlelight memorials, theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao's *Selected Works*, Deng Xiaoping's retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military ties). With near-zero followers and under 100 reposts, these generated no engagement or disruption. Bentham's utility calculus would demand scrutiny: What pleasure does society derive from punishing Chen? None evident—the "serious public disorder" alleged by Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun lacks empirical grounding, with no data on impact, causation, or even viewer metrics. The pain inflicted—over a year in prison, reputational ruin, and familial distress—far outweighs any preventive gain, violating Bentham's principle that punishment should be no more severe than necessary to deter. In *Rationale of Punishment* (1830), Bentham insists on proportionality: "The quantity of punishment... should be exactly sufficient" to outweigh the mischief. Here, the "pocket crime" vagueness amplifies pain without utility, a disproportionate lash that Bentham would decry as "barbarous" excess.

Moreover, the opacity of the process—non-public trial, denied full defenses, suppressed prison complaint letter—contradicts Bentham's emphasis on publicity as a cornerstone of justice. In *A Fragment on Government* (1776), he argues that secrecy breeds abuse, while open proceedings ensure accountability and deter corruption, maximizing societal happiness through informed public scrutiny. The selective enforcement—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—further erodes utility: it fosters inequality, where the "greatest number" (everyday citizens like Chen) bear the pain of arbitrary application, while elites evade it. Bentham's Panopticon was designed for equal visibility, not selective blindness; this case inverts it, turning law into a tool of hidden power, diminishing collective welfare by eroding trust in institutions.

Bentham's empiricism would also interrogate the ideological calculus: Chen's forwards, far from "insulting leaders," embody intellectual exchange that, in utilitarian terms, yields net pleasure—fostering informed discourse, cultural resilience, and societal self-correction. Suppressing them incurs pain not just for Chen but for the "greatest number," stifling the marketplace of ideas Bentham championed in his advocacy for free press. As he noted in *Plan for Parliamentary Reform* (1817), liberty of expression maximizes utility by enabling progress; here, the "high education = malice" presumption pathologizes inquiry, a Luddite regression that pains the body politic.

In conclusion, Chen's case is a utilitarian debacle: a calculus skewed toward elite security at the expense of widespread pain, devoid of preventive efficacy. Bentham would prescribe reform—transparent trials, evidence-based sentencing, and equality before law—to recalibrate toward maximum happiness. As China pursues comprehensive rule by law, this case serves as a cautionary sum: justice untethered from utility devolves into caprice, diminishing the felicity of the many. Reassessing such verdicts would not only right a wrong but amplify the greatest good—affirming Bentham's enduring dictum: "It is the greatest happiness of the greatest number that is the measure of right and wrong."
