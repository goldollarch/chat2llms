Saint Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), the preeminent medieval synthesizer of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology, offers a robust framework for evaluating political and legal matters through his doctrines of natural law, human law, and the common good. In the *Summa Theologica* (I-II, qq. 90–97), Aquinas defines law as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the community and promulgated." Eternal law (God's rational order) is reflected in natural law, accessible via human reason, which binds all and promotes virtue. Human law, derived from natural law, must serve justice and the common good; unjust laws lack binding force, as "an unjust law is no law at all" (ST I-II, q. 96, a. 4). In *On Kingship* (De Regno), Aquinas emphasizes that rulers, as stewards of divine order, must foster civic friendship, protect the innocent, and punish vice proportionally, harmonizing faith and reason for societal flourishing. Applied to Dr. Chen Jingyuan's 2023 conviction for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (寻衅滋事罪)—stemming from forwarding low-impact Twitter posts—this case starkly violates Thomistic principles, revealing a disconnect between human law and natural justice, and undermining the common good.

Aquinas's natural law posits that human acts must align with reason's inclination toward the good: self-preservation, procreation, social living, and rational pursuit of truth. Chen, an independent scholar specializing in complex systems physics, embodied this through his modest online activity: sharing artistic symbols (e.g., the "umbrella girl" cartoon evoking resilience), emotional expressions (e.g., June 4th candlelight memorials), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao's *Selected Works*, Deng Xiaoping's retirement endorsement). With near-zero followers and under 100 reposts, these actions posed no threat—mere exercises in rational inquiry, fostering civic discourse and mutual understanding, which Aquinas would deem virtuous under natural law's social precept (ST I-II, q. 94, a. 2). Yet, the Kunming judiciary—Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun—"sorted" them as "false information" insulting leaders and causing "serious public disorder," presuming Chen's "high education" implied malice. This inverts natural law: reason's pursuit of truth becomes criminalized, contradicting Aquinas's view that laws must aid, not hinder, the intellect's orientation to the divine good (ST I-II, q. 91, a. 2).

Human law, per Aquinas, derives validity from conformity to natural law; deviation renders it tyrannical. The "pocket crime" of "picking quarrels"—vague and expansively applied—exemplifies such invalidity. No evidence of causation (zero societal impact) or proportionality exists: posts lacked the "serious disorder" threshold (e.g., 500+ reposts per judicial guidelines), yet Chen received 20 months' imprisonment. Procedural flaws—non-public trial, denied defenses, suppressed prison complaint—breach the common good, as Aquinas insists rulers must ensure equitable processes to prevent "scandal" and foster trust (ST II-II, q. 33). Selective enforcement (state media like *Guangming Daily* republishing similar content unscathed) smacks of favoritism, violating the equality before law that natural justice demands (ST I-II, q. 96, a. 3). Aquinas would deem this "unjust law" non-binding, akin to tyrannical edicts that "oppress the people" (*On Kingship*, I.6), urging resistance through rational appeal or higher authority.

The case's broader implications betray the common good: Aquinas envisions polity as a "perfect community" (perfecta communitas) where laws promote virtue and peace (*On Kingship*, I.15). Suppressing scholarly discourse—Chen's forwards as micro-acts of rational mutuality—stifles the intellectual virtue of *scientia* (knowledge), essential for societal harmony. In a Thomistic polity, such inquiry aligns with natural law's truth-seeking, fostering "civic friendship" (ST II-II, q. 23). Instead, the judiciary's "high education = guilt" presumption pathologizes intellect, echoing Aquinas's caution against rulers who "pervert the law" for self-interest (*On Kingship*, I.6). Chen's *Prison Blood Letter*—a resolute defense invoking cognitive limits (Gödel's theorem) and vowing lifelong accountability—exemplifies Thomistic rectitude: facing injustice, he appeals to reason and higher law (eternal/natural), embodying the virtue of fortitude (ST II-II, q. 123).

In evaluation, the case fails Thomistic standards: human law deviates from natural justice, harming the common good and eroding civic virtue. Aquinas would advocate reform—aligning statutes with rational equity, ensuring proportionality, and protecting intellectual freedom—to restore legitimacy. Chen's ordeal, though unjust, highlights natural law's enduring voice: even amid tyranny, reason demands the good, urging rulers to heed divine order for true flourishing. As Aquinas concludes, "justice without mercy is cruelty; mercy without justice is license"—here, the scales tipped to cruelty, demanding Thomistic balance for a just polity.
