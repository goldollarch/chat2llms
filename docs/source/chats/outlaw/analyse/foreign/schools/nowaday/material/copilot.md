**From the perspective of New Materialism, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s case reveals how agency, resistance, and injustice are not merely human-centered phenomena but emerge from entangled material-discursive assemblages. His silence is not passive—it is a forceful act within a field of power, affect, and institutional apparatus.**

---

### 1. **Agency Beyond the Human: Silence as Material Force**

New Materialism challenges the anthropocentric notion that agency resides solely in human intention. Instead, agency is distributed across bodies, institutions, technologies, and discourses. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess—his silence—is not merely a personal decision. It is a *material act* shaped by and shaping the legal system, prison architecture, surveillance technologies, and political discourse.

His silence becomes a **vibrant matter** (Bennett) that resists capture. It unsettles the state’s narrative, disrupts the smooth functioning of coercive apparatuses, and generates affective ripples across civil society.

---

### 2. **Entanglement of Power and Matter**

New Materialists argue that power is not abstract—it is enacted through material arrangements. In Dr. Chen’s case, the charge of “picking quarrels and provoking trouble” is not just a linguistic label; it is embedded in **legal documents, courtroom rituals, detention cells, and surveillance systems**. These are not inert backdrops—they are active participants in the production of guilt.

Dr. Chen’s body, his written words, his silence—all become sites of **material-discursive entanglement**, where meaning and matter co-produce each other. His resistance is not outside the system—it is *within* and *against* its very textures.

---

### 3. **Affect and the Politics of Atmosphere**

New Materialism emphasizes the role of affect—non-conscious intensities that circulate between bodies and environments. Dr. Chen’s case generates an atmosphere of fear, outrage, and mourning. These affects are not secondary—they are **political forces** that shape public perception, mobilize solidarity, and destabilize dominant narratives.

His silence carries affective weight. It is not emptiness—it is **charged stillness**, a refusal that resonates across social and institutional boundaries.

---

### 4. **Refusing Representation: A Posthuman Ethics**

New Materialism critiques representationalism—the idea that language transparently reflects reality. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess is a refusal to be *represented* by the state’s categories. He resists being reduced to a “troublemaker,” a “criminal,” or a “case number.” His silence is a **posthuman ethical gesture**, asserting that identity and truth cannot be fully captured by institutional discourse.

This refusal is not nihilism—it is a **generative rupture**, opening space for new modes of being, knowing, and relating.

---

### 5. **Conclusion: Resistance as Material Practice**

From the standpoint of New Materialism, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s case is not merely a legal injustice—it is a **material drama of resistance**, where silence, affect, and entanglement challenge the state’s attempt to fix meaning and suppress agency. His body, his silence, his refusal—all participate in a **non-linear, distributed politics** that defies reduction.

> “Matter matters. And silence, too, is matter—dense with resistance, thick with meaning.” — New Materialist ethos

Dr. Chen’s silence is not absence—it is **presence**, a forceful material act within a field of coercion and care.
