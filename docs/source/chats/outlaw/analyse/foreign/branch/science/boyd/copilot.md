### 1. **Moral Realism: Ethical Truth Is Objective, Not Political**

Boyd’s moral realism asserts that ethical claims can be objectively true, much like scientific ones. Dr. Chen’s refusal to confess is a **moral stance grounded in truth**, not in political expediency. ---

### 2. **Epistemic Integrity: Against Ideological Distortion**

Boyd emphasizes that scientific and moral inquiry must resist ideological distortion. In Dr. Chen’s case, the legal system deploys vague and politically charged categories—“picking quarrels,” “provoking trouble”—that lack empirical and conceptual clarity. His silence is a **refusal to validate epistemically corrupt categories**, a protest against the manipulation of language to obscure truth.

He insists that **terms must track reality**, not serve power.

---

### 3. **Naturalistic Epistemology: Truth Emerges from Practice and Evidence**

Boyd’s naturalistic approach to knowledge stresses that truth arises from sustained engagement with the world—through evidence, reasoning, and principled inquiry. Dr. Chen’s work as a legal scholar reflects this ethos. His silence is not a withdrawal—it is a **continuation of his epistemic practice**, a refusal to speak falsely under duress.

His resistance is a **naturalistic affirmation of truth**, grounded in lived experience and intellectual rigor.

---

### 4. **Reference and Realism: Words Must Correspond to Reality**

Boyd’s semantic realism argues that our terms must refer to real entities and structures. The state’s use of abstract charges fails this test—it does not refer to any verifiable wrongdoing. Dr. Chen’s silence is a **semantic protest**, a refusal to let his name be attached to false referents.

He embodies the principle that **language must serve truth—not obscure it**.

---

### 5. **Conclusion: Silence as Epistemic Resistance**

From Richard Boyd’s philosophical perspective, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s silence is a **realist act of epistemic resistance**. It affirms the possibility of moral truth, rejects ideological distortion, and insists that justice must be grounded in reality—not in political fiction. His refusal to confess is not defiance—it is **a principled stand for the integrity of knowledge and the dignity of truth**.

> “Our best moral and scientific theories aim to describe reality as it is.” — *Richard Boyd (paraphrased)*

Dr. Chen’s silence is such a theory—**not spoken, but lived**, a refusal to betray the real in favor of the convenient.
