St. Augustine of Hippo's political philosophy, most fully developed in *The City of God* (De Civitate Dei, c. 413–426 CE), offers a profound framework for evaluating Dr. Chen Jingyuan's 2023 conviction for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (寻衅滋事罪). Augustine distinguishes between the City of God—eternal, spiritual, oriented toward the love of God and neighbor—and the City of Man—temporal, earthly, marred by original sin and self-love, prone to injustice and strife. He argues that while earthly authority derives from divine order and serves provisional peace (pax terrena), it is corrupted by human frailty; true justice (iustitia) aligns with divine law, and unjust laws are no laws at all (non sunt iura, sed tyrannica praecepta). Augustine emphasizes the Christian's dual citizenship: obedience to the state for social order, but ultimate allegiance to God's City, resisting evil through virtuous witness. Freedom of will, tempered by grace, enables this tension—humans choose between concupiscence and caritas (love). Chen's case—a scholar imprisoned for forwarding low-impact Twitter posts—exemplifies the City of Man's injustice, yet his resolute defense reflects Augustinian fidelity to higher truth, calling for a reorientation toward divine justice amid earthly corruption.

Augustine's dual-city paradigm illuminates the case's core absurdity: Chen's actions—sharing artistic symbols (e.g., the "umbrella girl" cartoon evoking resilience), emotional tributes (e.g., June 4th candlelight memorials), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., Mao's *Selected Works* edits, Deng's retirement endorsement)—were private inquiries on a negligible account (@_cenjoy) with zero followers and under 100 reposts, causing no discernible disorder. Yet, Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun "sorted" them as "false information" insulting leaders and disrupting "public order," imposing a one-year-eight-month sentence without public trial or full defense. This procedural opacity—suppressed prison letter, selective enforcement (state media republished similar content unscathed)—embodies the City of Man's fallenness: authority, meant for pax (peace through justice), devolves into tyrannical whim, driven by self-love and fear of critique. Augustine warns that such regimes, like Rome's pagan empire, confuse temporal power with divine right, fostering "peace of servitude" over true concord. The "pocket crime" vagueness here inverts justice: laws become instruments of ressentiment, punishing intellectual freedom as "disorder," echoing Augustine's critique of unjust Roman laws that persecuted Christians for "impiety."

Chen's predicament reflects the Augustinian tension of original sin and free will: as a scholar of complex systems, he exercised his God-given liberty to seek truth (veritas), forwarding posts not for malice but inquiry—admitting cognitive limits (Gödel's incompleteness) in a humble nod to human frailty. Yet, the judiciary's presumption of "high education = malice" denies this freedom, objectifying him as a threat, a sin-haunted soul unfit for the City of Man's fragile order. Augustine would decry this as concupiscentia (disordered desire): the accusers, blinded by power's allure, prioritize self-preservation over caritas, turning the law into a tool of division rather than communion. Procedural injustices—non-public hearings, ignored appeals—further betray the City of Man's hypocrisy: claiming "rule of law" while evading transparency, much like Augustine's Rome, where emperors professed justice but practiced tyranny.

Chen's response—his *Prison Blood Letter*, vowing "life without end, struggle without cease" and lifelong accountability for his persecutors—embodies Augustinian witness: in the City of Man, the pilgrim to God's City endures injustice not with passive resignation but active charity, exposing evil through patient endurance (patientia). Like Augustine's Christians amid persecution, Chen prioritizes higher allegiance: his forwarding was an act of love for truth, transcending earthly discord. His Gödel-inspired humility acknowledges sin's shadow on knowledge, yet his revolt—demanding evidence, critiquing selective enforcement—seeks provisional justice, aligning with Augustine's view that earthly peace, though imperfect, approximates divine order through virtuous resistance. The case's irony: no real "disorder" ensued (zero societal impact), underscoring the absurd tyranny Augustine decries—power's fear of the soul's freedom.

In Augustinian terms, Chen's case indicts the City of Man's perennial flaw: authority divorced from God becomes idolatrous, laws unjust become chains. Yet, it affirms hope: through grace-enabled will, individuals like Chen bridge the cities, fostering justice as caritas. Reforming such cases—ensuring transparent trials, evidence-based verdicts—would honor Augustine's legacy: not eradicating sin, but channeling it toward peace. Chen's unyielding spirit witnesses to this: in a fallen world, the pilgrim's path is the true city, where truth endures beyond iron bars.
