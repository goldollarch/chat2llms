An Existentialist Analysis of Dr. Chen Jingyuan's Case:
Sartrean Freedom, Bad Faith, and the Absurdity of Condemned Liberty

Jean-Paul Sartre's existentialism, as articulated in works like *Being and Nothingness* (1943) and *Existentialism is a Humanism* (1946), posits that "existence precedes essence": humans are "condemned to be free," thrown into an absurd world without predefined meaning or divine blueprint, tasked with creating their own essence through choices and actions. This freedom entails radical responsibility—no excuses, no appeals to fate or authority—yet it breeds anguish (angoisse), the nausea of realizing one's isolation and the weight of self-definition. Sartre distinguishes "authentic existence" (être pour-soi, being-for-itself), where one confronts freedom head-on, from "bad faith" (mauvaise foi), the self-deception of denying one's freedom by hiding behind roles or external forces. The "gaze of the Other" objectifies the self, reducing it to an in-itself (être-en-soi), a mere thing in another's eyes. Dr. Chen Jingyuan's case—a scholar imprisoned for forwarding innocuous Twitter posts—embodies these themes: a stark confrontation with absurd authority, a judicial apparatus steeped in bad faith, and Chen's resilient assertion of authentic freedom amid the gaze of state power.

Chen's actions—forwarding posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy) about art (e.g., the "umbrella girl" cartoon symbolizing resistance), emotion (e.g., June 4th candlelight memorials), theory (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and history (e.g., Mao's *Selected Works* edits, Deng Xiaoping's retirement endorsement)—are quintessentially Sartrean choices. With zero followers and under 100 reposts, these were not acts of mass disruption but private affirmations of his essence as an independent scholar pursuing truth through inquiry. Sartre would applaud this as "authentic existence": Chen, a physicist of complex systems, chose to engage global ideas not for external validation but to define himself against the void—collecting, critiquing, and preserving fragments of human discourse in an indifferent digital expanse. In forwarding these, he exercised his "facticity" (the given circumstances of his scholarly life) to forge "transcendence" (projecting meaning forward), refusing the inertia of conformity. Yet, this freedom was swiftly "condemned" by the judiciary: Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun branded it "picking quarrels and provoking trouble," a "pocket crime" fabricating "serious public disorder" from nothingness. Sartre's absurdity rings true here—the case's lack of impact (no quantifiable harm, no societal ripple) underscores the existential nausea: why punish a whisper in the void? It reveals the law not as rational essence but as arbitrary facticity, imposing meaning on the meaningless to mask its own groundlessness.

The judicial process exemplifies Sartre's "bad faith," a collective self-deception where actors deny their freedom by invoking roles and external justifications. Pu's presumption that Chen's "high education" implies he "should know" the posts were "false and insulting" is pure mauvaise foi: it evades the responsibility to prove intent (mens rea) by hiding behind the "object" of education, reducing Chen to a predefined "intellectual threat." Ge and Li perpetuate this by "sorting" posts without evidence—art as "rumors," theory as "attacks"—denying their own freedom to question the narrative of "national security." This is the gaze of the Other writ large: the state's "condemning look" objectifies Chen, stripping his subjective freedom and fixing him as an "in-itself" criminal, a mere cog in the disorder machine. Sartre describes the gaze as turning the self into an "object of shame"; here, it's an object of fabricated guilt, where procedural opacity (non-public trial, suppressed prison letter) enforces isolation, amplifying anguish. The selective enforcement—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—further unmasks bad faith: the judiciary plays at universality while serving partial interests, a hypocritical "civilized monster" echoing Sartre's critique of bourgeois morality.

Yet, Chen's response—his *Prison Blood Letter*, vowing "life without end, struggle without cease" and lifelong accountability for his accusers—embodies authentic existence amid absurdity. Like Sartre's Roquentin in *Nausea*, Chen confronts the contingency of his plight (a scholar jailed for whispers) without fleeing into self-deception. He owns his choices, admitting cognitive limits (Gödel's incompleteness, Humean skepticism) yet affirming them as scholarly essence. His refusal to beg mercy, instead exposing systemic contradictions (e.g., high education as both virtue and vice), mirrors Sartre's call to "choose oneself" responsibly. In the gaze of power, Chen inverts objectification: by writing from iron bars, he reclaims transcendence, turning imprisonment into a project of truth-seeking. This is Sartrean commitment (engagement): not passive victimhood, but active resistance, recognizing that freedom persists even in chains—one must invent meaning against the absurd.

Sartrean existentialism thus illuminates Chen's case as a microcosm of condemned liberty: a scholar's free inquiry crushed by bad-faith authority, yet resilient in its absurdity. It critiques the judiciary's self-deceptive roles, urging a turn from objectifying gazes to authentic dialogue. In a world without essence, Chen's unyielding struggle affirms: we are free to choose resistance, bearing the anguish as our essence. His case, like Sartre's humanism, reminds us: hell may be other people, but salvation lies in owning one's freedom, no matter the bars.
