A Commentary on the Chen Jingyuan Case: Insights from Robert Alexy's Non-Positivist Legal Theory

Robert Alexy's legal philosophy, particularly as developed in *A Theory of Legal Argumentation* (1978/1989) and *A Theory of Fundamental Rights* (1985/2002), offers a robust framework for critiquing the Chen Jingyuan case, illuminating the tensions between positivist legal formalism and the normative demands of justice. Alexy, a leading non-positivist, rejects the strict separation of law and morality, arguing that law is a "discourse-dependent" system where validity requires moral argumentation. Rights, in his view, function as "principles"—optimization requirements that demand balancing in concrete cases to achieve the best possible moral outcome. This balancing test, comprising suitability (does the measure advance a goal?), necessity (is it the least restrictive means?), and proportionality stricto sensu (do benefits outweigh burdens?), ensures that legal decisions align with the "ideal of a democratic and constitutional state." Alexy's emphasis on argumentative rules—universalizability, justification, and sincerity—further underscores that judicial reasoning must transcend mere application of rules to engage substantive justice. Applied to Chen's 2023 conviction for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (寻衅滋事罪) under Article 293 of the PRC Criminal Law, the case exemplifies a failure of moral-legal discourse, where procedural opacity and disproportionate punishment undermine fundamental rights like freedom of expression.

Chen Jingyuan, an independent scholar with a Ph.D. in physics specializing in complex systems, was sentenced to 20 months' imprisonment by Kunming's Xishan District Court (Judge Pu Huijun presiding) for forwarding Twitter posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy). These included artistic cartoons (e.g., the "umbrella girl" symbolizing protest resilience), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical discussions (e.g., political spectrum analyses, evaluations of Xi Jinping, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and historical materials (e.g., edits to Mao's *Selected Works*, retired cadre pay slips, Deng Xiaoping's retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military cooperation). With near-zero followers and under 100 reposts—yielding negligible engagement—the prosecution claimed these constituted "false information" insulting leaders and causing "serious public disorder," per the 2013 Supreme People's Court and Supreme People's Procuratorate Interpretation on network defamation. The non-public trial, denied defenses, and suppressed prison complaint letter compounded the procedural flaws, with appeals summarily rejected by the Intermediate Court (Judge Li Xiangyun).

Alexy's principles theory directly indicts this as a rights violation. Freedom of expression, a cornerstone principle in constitutional democracies (and echoed in Article 35 of the PRC Constitution), collides here with the state's interest in "social order." Alexy mandates balancing: first, suitability—did Chen's actions advance disorder? Empirical data (zero societal impact) suggests no; the posts were private scholarly notes, not incitement. Second, necessity—were less restrictive alternatives (e.g., administrative warnings) unavailable? The judiciary's leap to criminal sanction, without evidence of intent or harm, fails this test. Third, proportionality stricto sensu—the 20-month sentence burdens Chen's expressive freedom disproportionately against a non-existent benefit, inverting Alexy's optimization imperative: rights should be maximized, not minimized by arbitrary fiat.

Moreover, the case flouts Alexy's argumentative rules. Judicial reasoning must be universalizable (could the same logic apply generally without absurdity?) and justified through sincere discourse. Pu's presumption that Chen's "high education" implies "knowledge of falsity" is neither: it universalizes to condemn all intellectuals for inquiry, fostering hypocrisy over sincerity. The "sorting" of posts as "rumors" without appraisals or data exemplifies non-discursive positivism—mere rule application devoid of moral weighing. Alexy critiques such "claim to correctness" failures: law claims moral validity but must substantiate it; here, selective enforcement (state media republishing similar content unscathed) reveals insincerity, undermining the democratic rule of law.

In Alexy's non-positivist vision, law is a "practical discourse" bridging is and ought, where judges act as moral reasoners. The Chen case, with its procedural voids (e.g., denied self-defense, per Article 14 of the Criminal Procedure Law), perverts this into authoritarian monologue. Yet, Chen's *Prison Blood Letter*—a resolute appeal invoking cognitive limits (Gödel's theorem) and systemic critique—embodies Alexian sincerity: a call for argumentative redemption, demanding the state justify its "order" against expressive liberty.

Ultimately, Alexy's framework condemns the case as a betrayal of constitutional morality: without balancing and discourse, law devolves into coercion, eroding the "ideal" of justice. Reforming such "pocket crimes" through principled weighing would realign with Alexy's vision, ensuring rights optimization in China's rule-of-law pursuit. Chen's ordeal, though unjust, spotlights the path: from positivist rigidity to discursive humanity.
