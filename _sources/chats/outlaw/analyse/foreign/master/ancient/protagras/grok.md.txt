An Evaluation of the Chen Jingyuan Case Based on Protagoras's Philosophical Core Ideas

Protagoras of Abdera (c. 490-420 BCE), the pre-Socratic sophist and teacher of rhetoric, encapsulated his philosophy in the maxim "Man is the measure of all things" (*anthropos metron panton*), asserting radical relativism: truth and value are subjective, determined by individual perception rather than absolute standards. His core ideas, as preserved in Plato's *Protagoras* and *Theaetetus*, include the impossibility of objective knowledge (perceptions vary, so "hot" to one is "cold" to another); rhetoric (*techne logon*) as the art of persuasive speech to navigate social realities; and humanism, emphasizing education and civic virtue (*arete*) as tools for personal and communal flourishing in a contingent world. Protagoras critiqued dogmatic certainty, advocating adaptability and dialogue over imposed universals. The Chen Jingyuan case—a doctoral scholar sentenced to 20 months for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (PRC Criminal Law Article 293) over Twitter forwards—through Protagoras's lens, exemplifies the tyranny of imposed measures: the judiciary's absolute "order" denies subjective perception, suppressing rhetorical adaptability and humanistic inquiry, reducing justice to a relativistic farce.

Protagoras would decry this as sophistic inversion: justice, measured by civic appearances, yields to ruler's yardstick—selective enforcement (millions unpunished) exposes the relativity, where "threat" measures only in power's eye.
