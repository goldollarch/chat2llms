A Schmittian Critique of the Chen Jingyuan Case: 
Sovereignty, the Friend-Enemy Distinction, and the Political Decision in Law

Carl Schmitt's political jurisprudence, as articulated in *The Concept of the Political* (1927) and *Political Theology* (1922), radically reorients our understanding of law and state away from liberal neutralism toward the primacy of political decision. For Schmitt, the essence of the political is the friend-enemy distinction: politics crystallizes in the existential grouping of collectives into friends (allies) and enemies (existential threats), where enmity is not moral or economic but a concrete possibility of physical killing. Sovereignty, in turn, is "the decision on the exception"—the sovereign's capacity to suspend norms in moments of crisis, revealing law not as autonomous but as suspended by political will. Liberal constitutionalism, with its emphasis on rules and rights, masks this reality, pretending politics can be depoliticized into administration. Schmitt warns that such illusions weaken the state, inviting true enmity to exploit the gaps. Dr. Chen Jingyuan's 2023 conviction for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (寻衅滋事罪)—a vague "pocket crime" for forwarding innocuous Twitter posts—vividly illustrates Schmitt's theses: a sovereign decision framing a scholar as enemy, suspending legal norms under the guise of order, and exposing the fragility of rule-of-law pretensions in the face of political enmity.

Chen's case begins with seemingly apolitical acts: on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy), he forwarded posts totaling under 100 reposts with zero followers—artistic cartoons (e.g., the "umbrella girl" symbolizing resistance), emotional memorials (e.g., June 4th candlelight images), theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., Mao's *Selected Works* edits, Deng Xiaoping's retirement endorsement). These are fragments of discourse, not manifestos; their engagement was negligible, causing no measurable "serious public disorder." Yet, Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun "sorted" them as "false information" insulting leaders and disrupting order, imposing a one-year-and-eight-month sentence without public trial or full defense. Schmitt would see this not as legal error but as a sovereign decision on the exception: the posts, innocuous in isolation, become "enemy" symbols—subversive whispers threatening the friend-collective of national unity. The "picking quarrels" charge, with its elastic vagueness, suspends normative precision, allowing the state to decide enmity ad hoc. As Schmitt notes, "The specific political distinction... is that between friend and enemy," and here, Chen—a scholar of complex systems—is cast as the existential other, his high education presumed as "malice" proof, inverting intellectual freedom into threat.

This decision unmasks the liberal illusion Schmitt decries: the pretense that law operates in a depoliticized sphere of rights and procedures. Chen's non-public hearing, denied defenses, and suppressed prison letter exemplify the exception's sovereignty—the state suspends due process to "protect order," revealing law as politically contingent. Schmitt argues that "norms are valid only if they can be enforced," but in Chen's case, enforcement precedes validity: "disorder" is decided first, norms retrofitted. Selective application—state media like Guangming Daily republishing similar content unscathed—highlights the friend-enemy asymmetry: allies within the collective are immune, while perceived outsiders like Chen are othered. This is Schmitt's critique of liberalism incarnate: its "neutral" rules (e.g., presumption of innocence) crumble when politics intrudes, as the sovereign must decide the exception to preserve the political unity.

Yet, Schmitt's lens also illuminates resistance's limits and potentials. Chen's *Prison Blood Letter*—vowing "life without end, struggle without cease" and lifelong accountability—asserts a counter-sovereignty: refusing the enemy's framing, he redefines enmity as the judiciary's "black gang" (司法黑帮), perverting law into private vendetta. In Schmittian terms, this is a rival decision on the exception, challenging the state's monopoly on enmity. However, Schmitt's conservatism tempers optimism: true politics requires a sovereign collective, and individual revolt risks absorption into the "they" of liberal discourse. Chen's scholarly essence—questioning via Gödel's limits and complexity theory—evokes a Nietzschean-Schmittian revaluation, but without collective friend-enemy solidarity, it remains fragmented.

Schmittian analysis thus frames Chen's case as a sovereign theater: the state's decision on enmity suspends law, exposing constitutionalism's fragility. It warns that ignoring political essence invites true disruption—perhaps the "avalanche" Chen predicts from judicial overreach. For Schmitt, salvation lies not in rights but in decisive politics: reassert sovereignty to distinguish friends from foes authentically. Chen's ordeal, a microcosm of this, urges a Schmittian reckoning: define enmity clearly, or risk endless exceptions eroding the political itself. In the end, the case is less about one scholar than the sovereign's gaze—deciding who is enemy, and at what cost to the state's very Being.
