---

### 1. **Truth Exists Independently of Belief**

Realism holds that truth is not contingent on perception, consensus, or authority. In Dr. Chen’s case, the injustice he resists is not a matter of opinion—it is an **objective moral wrong**, regardless of how the state frames it. His refusal to confess is a rejection of **epistemic relativism**, a declaration that truth does not bend to power.

---

### 2. **Moral Facts Are Real**

Moral realism asserts that ethical values—such as justice, dignity, and human rights—are not social constructs but **real features of the world**. Dr. Chen’s resistance is grounded in this conviction. He does not negotiate or compromise because he believes that **some principles are inviolable**, not because they are useful or popular, but because they are true.

His silence is a form of **moral realism in action**—a refusal to betray what is right for what is expedient.

---

### 3. **Against Constructivism: Law Is Not Whatever Power Says It Is**

Realism challenges legal and political constructivism—the idea that institutions create truth through language and procedure. Dr. Chen’s case exposes the danger of this view: when law becomes a tool of repression, it loses its connection to **real justice**.

His refusal to participate in the legal fiction of guilt is a **realist protest**. He insists that law must correspond to truth, not merely enact power.

---

### 4. **The Reality of Injustice**

Realism demands that we confront the **reality of suffering and injustice**, not explain it away through ideology or relativism. Dr. Chen’s silence forces observers to reckon with the **fact** of repression—not as a narrative, but as a **moral and ontological reality**.

His case is not a story—it is a **truth-event**, a moment where reality breaks through the veil of institutional denial.

---

### 5. **Conclusion: Silence as Fidelity to the Real**

From a realist perspective, Dr. Chen Jingyuan’s silence is a **philosophical affirmation of reality**. It refuses to participate in the distortion of truth, the relativization of justice, or the erasure of dignity. His stance is not symbolic—it is **ontological**, grounded in the conviction that truth exists, and that fidelity to it is the highest ethical calling.

> “To be silent in the face of falsehood is not to evade truth—it is to protect it.” — *Realist ethos*

Dr. Chen’s silence protects truth. It affirms that **what is real remains real**, even when denied, and that **justice must begin with recognition of the world as it is**, not as power wishes it to be.
