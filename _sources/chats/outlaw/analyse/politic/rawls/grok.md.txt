Justice as Fairness and the Chen Jingyuan Case: A Rawlsian Critique

John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) remains a cornerstone of contemporary political philosophy, articulating a conception of justice as fairness that prioritizes the protection of basic liberties and the equitable distribution of social burdens and benefits. At its heart lies the "original position," a hypothetical thought experiment where rational agents, veiled from knowledge of their personal circumstances (the "veil of ignorance"), design principles for a just society. From this vantage, Rawls derives two principles of justice: first, the principle of equal liberty, ensuring that each person enjoys the most extensive basic liberties compatible with a similar system of liberty for others; second, the difference principle, permitting inequalities only if they maximize the position of the least advantaged and ensure fair equality of opportunity. These principles safeguard not only individual rights but also the institutional frameworks—like impartial judiciary and free expression—that enable human flourishing. Applied to Dr. Chen Jingyuan's 2023 conviction for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (寻衅滋事罪) under China's legal system, the case starkly illustrates a profound failure of justice as fairness, where the veil of ignorance is torn asunder by arbitrary power, basic liberties are curtailed, and the least advantaged—intellectual dissenters like Chen—are systematically disadvantaged.

Rawls's equal liberty principle demands robust protection of fundamental freedoms, including freedom of thought, expression, and association, as these are prerequisites for self-respect and democratic participation. In the original position, no rational agent would risk endorsing a system that criminalizes private intellectual inquiry, for anyone might find themselves in the position of the marginalized thinker. Chen, an independent scholar with a Ph.D. in physics specializing in complex systems, exemplifies this: his forwarding of Twitter posts on his low-profile account (@_cenjoy)—artistic symbols like the "umbrella girl" cartoon evoking resilience, emotional memorials such as June 4th candlelight images, theoretical debates (e.g., political spectrum analyses, evaluations of Xi Jinping, Trump's critique of communism, Pompeo's U.S.-China remarks), and historical facts (e.g., edits to Mao's *Selected Works*, retired cadre pay slips, Deng Xiaoping's retirement endorsement, Sino-Ukrainian military cooperation)—posed no discernible threat. With zero followers and under 100 reposts yielding negligible engagement, these acts were solitary affirmations of curiosity, not public agitation. Yet, Judge Pu Huijun, Prosecutor Ge Bin, and appellate Judge Li Xiangyun "sorted" them as "false information" insulting leaders and causing "serious public disorder," imposing a one-year-and-eight-month sentence without public trial or full defense rights. This violates Rawlsian liberty: speech is not a dispensable commodity but a basic right, essential for the "circulation of moral and political ideas" that Rawls deems vital for a well-ordered society. Under the veil of ignorance, no one would consent to a regime where forwarding historical documents or philosophical critiques—harmless in isolation—triggers imprisonment, as it disproportionately burdens the intellectually curious, the least advantaged in an authoritarian context.

The difference principle compounds the injustice, as the case entrenches inequalities that do not benefit the worst-off. Rawls requires that social institutions arrange inequalities to maximize the prospects of the least advantaged, ensuring fair equality of opportunity through open access to education, expression, and justice. Chen's scholarly background—rising from a humble Yunnan family to expertise in complex systems—represents opportunity realized through merit, yet the judiciary inverts this: his "high education" becomes presumptive evidence of malice, a perverse "sorting" that disadvantages the educated dissenter. Selective enforcement exacerbates this: state media like *Guangming Daily* and Sina republished similar content without repercussion, while Chen, a powerless independent, faces iron bars. This is not fair equality of opportunity but a rigged game, where the powerful (institutional voices) thrive, and the least advantaged (lone scholars) bear the burdens of "order." Rawls's maximin rule—prioritizing the worst-off—condemns such disparities: a just society would protect vulnerable inquirers like Chen, fostering the "strains of commitment" to liberty that bind citizens in mutual respect. Instead, procedural opacity (suppressed prison letter, non-public hearings) and fabricated causality (no data on "disorder" from zero-impact posts) entrench inequality, turning justice into a tool for the advantaged.

Rawls's framework also illuminates the institutional failures: a just basic structure requires an independent judiciary to enforce principles impartially, yet Chen's case reveals capture by non-ideal forces—perhaps political pressures—eroding the "circumstances of justice." Under the veil, agents would design safeguards against such biases, ensuring trials are public, defenses robust, and burdens of proof on the state. Chen's *Prison Blood Letter*, invoking scientific limits (Gödel's incompleteness) and vowing lifelong accountability for his accusers, embodies Rawlsian self-respect: refusing bad faith, he demands the fair terms of cooperation denied him.

In sum, Chen's ordeal contravenes Rawls's justice as fairness, curtailing liberties and exacerbating inequalities without benefit to the least advantaged. It calls for institutional reform: transparent procedures, robust free speech protections, and egalitarian opportunity to align with the original position's impartiality. As Rawls envisioned, a just society is one where the least advantaged could affirm its principles from behind the veil—Chen's case, a poignant failure, urges us toward that ideal.
