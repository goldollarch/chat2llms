An Evaluation of the Chen Jingyuan Case Based on Huang Zongxi's Historical and Philosophical Views

Huang Zongxi (1607-1695), a Ming loyalist and Qing-era historian, developed a philosophy of history and governance in *Mingyi Dai Fang Lu* (Waiting for the Dawn, c. 1663), critiquing imperial despotism as the root of dynastic decay. His core ideas include the "public sphere" (*gong*) over monarchical whim (*si*), where scholars (*shidaifu*) act as vigilant stewards of collective interest, preventing ruler tyranny through constitutional checks like advisory assemblies and merit-based administration. Huang viewed history as cyclical moral decline (*daoli*), driven by unchecked power eroding ethical norms, advocating intellectual freedom as the antidote—scholars must preserve cultural vitality through critical reflection, not subservience. The Chen Jingyuan case—a doctoral scholar sentenced to 20 months for "picking quarrels and provoking trouble" (PRC Criminal Law Article 293) over Twitter forwards—through Huang's lens, exemplifies despotic overreach: the judiciary's "order" subordinates the public sphere to private fiat, suppressing scholarly stewardship and accelerating historical decline, betraying the moral cycle's call for vigilant reform.

#### 1. Despotism and the Erosion of the Public Sphere: Judicial Fiat as Tyrannical Particularism

Huang's critique of despotism posits absolute monarchy as "private interest" (*si*) devouring the "public good" (*gong*), where unchecked power fragments society into ruler's whim versus collective welfare.

Article 293's application embodies this erosion: presuming "high education implies discernment" pathologizes Chen's forwards (e.g., Hayek critiques as economic reflection or the "Trump-kneeling Xi" cartoon as satirical vigilance) as private "disruption," subordinating public discourse to state fiat. The closed-door trial and "shut up" directive amplify particularism: Chen's prison letter—stewarding *gong* through taxonomy (art/emotion/reason/fact) and avalanche theory—exemplifies scholarly check, yet is silenced as "resistance." Huang, who decried Ming eunuch tyranny, would decry this as cyclical peril: evidentiary voids (prosecutor's unverified admission) expose the whim's fragility, yet selective enforcement (millions unpunished) fragments the sphere, inverting reform's telos into despotic consolidation.

#### 2. The Scholar's Stewardship and Intellectual Freedom: Suppressed Inquiry as Moral Vigilance Betrayed

Huang envisioned scholars as moral guardians (*shidaifu*), wielding independent critique to sustain historical continuity and ethical norms, free from ruler's shadow.

The sentence betrays this guardianship: interpellating Chen as "quarrelsome" subjugates vigilant inquiry to "order," as the barred taxonomy denies moral synthesis—reflecting Huang's call for scholars to "rectify names" against decay. The non-oral appeal perpetuates betrayal: Chen's theory, linking non-linear flux to historiographical vigilance, is dismissed, echoing Huang's lament for silenced Ming reformers. This suppression risks ethical atrophy: without scholarly freedom, vigilance wanes, as in Huang's view of dynastic fall—evidentiary anomalies signal the cycle's turning, yet unheeded, fracturing continuity.

#### 3. Historical Cycles and Moral Decline: Coercive "Order" as Acceleration of Despotic Ruin

Huang's cyclical historiography traces dynasties' rise and fall to moral erosion: unchecked despotism breeds corruption, redeemable by public-oriented reform.

The 20-month fiat accelerates decline: "upper-level instructions" embody unchecked whim, corrupting the cycle—Huang would see in selective voids a harbinger of ruin, as unpunished masses harbor latent reform. The case's opacity risks terminal fall: without scholarly rectification, *daoli* (moral way) dissolves, inverting rejuvenation into stagnation.

#### Conclusion: Huang's Lens on the Case—A Despotic Cycle Betraying Scholarly Guardianship

From Huang Zongxi's anti-despotic historiography, the Chen Jingyuan case is a moral nadir: public sphere eroded, guardianship betrayed, and decline accelerated, dimming reform's light. As of October 24, 2025, no retrial or exoneration has occurred; Chen's account remains dormant, its quiet a vigilant ember. This case cautions: without *gong*'s stewardship, despotism devours. As Huang proclaimed, "The ruler serves the public"—may the cycle yet turn.
